\title{Choptuik's echo period at large dimension: evidence for a finite limit
$\Delta_\infty = 1.64389375$, and selection beyond all orders in $1/D$}

\author{Nathan Moinvaziri}
\email{nathan@nathanm.com}
\affiliation{Independent researcher}
\date{October 2026; unreviewed draft}

\begin{abstract}
We compute the echo period $\Delta(D)$ of Choptuik's discretely self-similar critical solution of massless scalar
collapse in $D$ spacetime dimensions at 857 points $8.25 \le D \le 61.03$ with a double-double (about 32-digit)
solver, consistent with smooth curves to $4\times10^{-15}$, and with an independent double-precision solver for
$4 \le D \le 40$. One rational function of $1/(D-2)$ reproduces $\Delta$ and the critical exponent $\gamma$ for all
$D \ge 4$ within the precision of the points. Series in $1/D$ fitted on $D \le 45$ predict the points up to $D = 60.1$ to
$5\times10^{-13}$ (to $D = 61$: $7\times10^{-13}$), 40 to 300 times better than series in half-integer powers; this supports, but does not prove,
analyticity in $1/D$, which then gives $\Dinf = 1.64389375 \pm 1.5\times10^{-8}$ (the spread over 48 fit choices); the
critical exponent, computed in double-double up to $D = 61$, tends to $\gamma_\infty = 1/2$ to about $5\times10^{-7}$. We explain
this limit, given the conjectured selection rule below and the uniqueness of the unstable mode: at $D = \infty$ an unstable
mode with $\lambda = 1/\gamma = 2$ is generated by an exact symmetry, a shift of $r^2$, of the leading-order large-$D$
equations of Rozali and Way. A power-law approach to $\Delta = 0$ with exponent at least $0.01$, as conjectured in the
literature, is excluded by the data; a logarithmic one is disfavoured. We show that, within the scaling measured on the
solutions, the $1/D$ expansion of the self-similar field equations can be carried to all orders for any period and any
time profile, so the period (and likewise $\gamma$) is selected beyond all orders in $1/D$. Without gravity the obstruction is a
closed-form light-cone coefficient, which we identify as the Stokes multiplier of a mode born at large self-similar
radius. We conjecture that the selection rule at $D = \infty$ is the vanishing of the Stokes multipliers of an
$n$-free inner problem; its higher-harmonic conditions give the shape of the echo, $|F_3/F_1| = 0.0551$, in agreement
with the data. Truncated to three harmonics, the full set of conditions selects $\Delta = 1.619$, 1.5\% below
$\Dinf$, and with five harmonics $\Delta = 1.64516$, 0.077\% above (one harmonic: 29\% above).
Without truncation, an amplitude expansion to twelve orders selects $\omega = (1.0000 \pm 0.0001)\,\omega_\infty$ (the
range of several estimators, not an error bound) at
amplitude 2.720 (measured: 2.70--2.72). The conjectured rule thus gives $\Dinf = 1.6439 \pm 0.0002$ from the
$D = \infty$ equations alone, against $1.64389375$ from the solutions; the rule itself is not yet proved.
The computations, derivations and text were produced by an AI model (Claude, Anthropic) under the author's
direction and have not yet been reviewed by a human expert (Sec.~\ref{sec:how}).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Fine-tuning a one-parameter family of initial data for a massless scalar field to the threshold of black-hole
formation, Choptuik found that the evolution approaches a universal critical solution that repeats itself on ever
smaller scales: it is discretely self-similar (DSS) with echo period $\Delta$, and black holes formed just above
threshold have radius $r_{\rm BH} \propto (p - p^*)^\gamma$~\cite{Choptuik1993}; in $D$ dimensions their mass scales as
$M \propto (p - p^*)^{(D-3)\gamma}$, which in four dimensions is the familiar $M \propto (p - p^*)^\gamma$. The scaling follows from
the critical solution having a single unstable mode, with $\gamma = 1/\lambda$ for its growth rate
$\lambda$~\cite{KoikeHaraAdachi1995}. Throughout, $\gamma$ is this radius exponent. In four dimensions
$\Delta = 3.445452402(3)$~\cite{MartinGarciaGundlach2003} and $\gamma \simeq 0.374$~\cite{Gundlach1997}; see the
review~\cite{GundlachReview2025}.

The dependence on the spacetime dimension $D$ has been studied numerically, mostly at percent-level precision, for
$3.5 \le D \le 14$ (mostly integer $D$)~\cite{GarfinkleCutlerDuncan1999,SorkinOren2005,BlandEtAl2005,BlandKunstatter2007,Bland2007,TavesKunstatter2011}, and to about
$10^{-6}$ for continuous $3.05 < D < 5.5$ by Ecker, Ecker, Grumiller and Jechtl~\cite{EckerEtAl2026}, who find a
maximum of $\Delta$ near $D = 3.756$. Ecker, Ecker and Grumiller~\cite{EckerEckerGrumiller2026} constructed analytic
DSS solutions at large $D$ and, with~\cite{EckerEtAl2026}, conjectured $\Delta \to 0$ as $D \to \infty$; they also report the
expectation $\gamma \to 1/2$, which goes back to Rozali and Way~\cite{RozaliWay2018}. Rozali and Way derived the leading-order
large-$D$ equations of a self-gravitating scalar field, observed that they are parabolic with the roles of space and time
swapped, and estimated $\gamma = 1/2$ from the divergence of their oscillating solutions. Clark and
Pimentel~\cite{ClarkPimentel2025} constructed continuously self-similar critical solutions at large $D$ by matched
asymptotics. Large $D$ has been a fruitful limit in gravity~\cite{EmparanHerzog2020}.

This paper reports the following.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\Delta(D)$ at 857 points $8.25 \le D \le 61.03$ from a double-double solver, and $\Delta$, $\gamma$ for
$4 \le D \le 40$ from a double-precision solver; one rational formula in $1/(D-2)$ for all $D \ge 4$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:results}). It reproduces the published values at $D = 4$ and the maximum of $\Delta$ below $D = 4$; at
$D = 5$ it differs from~\cite{EckerEtAl2026} by $6\times10^{-5}$, a difference that the published higher-resolution
data of~\cite{EckerEtAl2026} trace to resolution.
\item Held-out tests that support analyticity of $\Delta$ in $1/D$, giving $\Dinf = 1.64389375 \pm 1.5\times10^{-8}$; a
power-law approach to zero is excluded and a logarithmic one disfavoured; given analyticity, nine constants tested as
closed forms for $\Dinf$ are excluded (Sec.~\ref{sec:limit}).
\item The $1/D$ expansion never fixes the period (Proposition~\ref{prop:1}); the leading-order problem and its
light-cone coefficient in closed form (Proposition~\ref{prop:2}); what gravity does to the non-analytic piece
(Proposition~\ref{prop:3}); and a proof that the late orders of the $1/D$ series encode that coefficient as a Stokes multiplier
(Proposition~\ref{prop:4}) (Sec.~\ref{sec:theory}); and an exact unstable mode with $\lambda = 2$ at $D = \infty$, from a shift
symmetry of $r^2$ of Rozali and Way's leading-order equations, which explains $\gamma_\infty = 1/2$ under the selection
conjecture and the uniqueness of the unstable mode (Proposition~\ref{prop:5}).
\item An $n$-free inner problem whose Stokes multipliers, by a conjecture stated with its hypotheses and the points where
a proof is missing (Conjecture~\ref{conj}), select the period; its
higher-harmonic conditions give $|F_3/F_1| = 0.0551$ and $|F_5/F_1| = 0.00314$ for the echo profile at
$D = \infty$, in agreement with $0.0549$--$0.0552$ and $0.0032$--$0.0034$ extrapolated from the solutions
(Sec.~\ref{sec:inner}). The harmonic-1 condition, which would give $\Dinf$, is not yet settled: truncated to three
harmonics, the conditions select $\omega = 1.0156\,\omega_\infty$ with the measured shape, with five about
$0.99923\,\omega_\infty$, and without
truncation, at twelve orders, $(1.0000 \pm 0.0001)\,\omega_\infty$ at amplitude 2.72 (measured: 2.70--2.72).
\item An analysis of the next-to-leading-order condition of~\cite{EckerEckerGrumiller2026}, which within this framework
does not select $\Delta$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:eeg}).
\end{enumerate}
The inner equations of Sec.~\ref{sec:inner} are, in other variables, the leading-order large-$D$ system of Rozali and
Way~\cite{RozaliWay2018}, who also noted its heat-equation character and estimated $\gamma = 1/2$; what is new here is
their use for the discretely self-similar solution: the selection rule and the symmetry argument of
Proposition~\ref{prop:5}.

\section{Equations and numerical method}
\label{sec:method}

We use the self-similar radius $x$ (radius over time to the singularity, normalized so that the past light cone of
the singularity is at $x = 1$) and $\tau = -\ln(-t)$, or the phase $\theta = \omega\tau$ with $\omega = 2\pi/\Delta$.
With $n = D - 2$, $p$ and $m$ the (scaled) derivatives of the scalar field along the two radial null directions, $a$
the radial metric function ($b = a^2$) and $c$ the lapse-type function that sets the light cones, the equations are
\begin{align}
x c_x &= (n-1)\,c\,(b-1), &
x b_x &= b\,(p^2+m^2) - (n-1)\,b\,(b-1), \label{eq:E12}\\
x(x-c)\,p_x &= -\omega x\,p_\theta + G p - c p + \tfrac{n}{2}\,c\,(p+m), &
x(x+c)\,m_x &= -\omega x\,m_\theta - G m + c m - \tfrac{n}{2}\,c\,(p+m), \label{eq:E34}
\end{align}
with $G = (n-1)\,c\,(b-1)$, on $0 \le x \le 1$; $p$, $m$ contain only odd harmonics of $\theta$ and $b$, $c$ only even
ones; the solution is regular at $x = 0$, analytic at the past light cone $x = 1$, and $c(1) = 1$ fixes the gauge.

\emph{Double-precision solver} ($4 \le D \le 40$): spectral multiple shooting in $x$ with Fourier modes in $\tau$
(23--47 modes), with the integration-step error extrapolated out; near $D = 4$ the points are extrapolated in the
mode count. Above $D \approx 26$ the period is selected by an effect that weakens exponentially with $D$, and rounding
at $10^{-16}$ moves $\Delta$ by $10^{-7}$--$10^{-6}$ at $D = 30$--$40$.

\emph{Double-double solver} ($8.25 \le D \le 61.03$): double-double arithmetic (about 32 digits) on SIMD vectors;
Taylor series in $x$ of order 36 with truncation $10^{-32}$; centre and horizon series to $10^{-33}$; collocation in
$\tau$ on the half period (32 points); an exact Jacobian by forward-mode duals with a double-double LU; continuation in
$D$ with deflated Newton steps. The Jacobian has a ladder of near-null directions (high $\tau$-harmonics of the centre
data, singular values falling roughly like $e^{-0.7k}$); from $D = 35$ the centre harmonics above 13 are pinned, which
moves $\Delta$ by $5\times10^{-15}$ at $D = 35$ and $3\times10^{-17}$ at $D = 40$. Checks are listed in
Table~\ref{tab:checks}. The 857 points fit smooth curves to about $4\times10^{-15}$; the estimated error per point is
about $10^{-16}$ to $D = 60.1$ and a few $10^{-15}$ above, where the tolerance was relaxed.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{One formula for all $D \ge 4$}

With $\eps = 1/n = 1/(D-2)$,
\begin{equation}
\Delta(D) = \frac{\sum_{k=0}^{8} a_k \eps^k}{1 + b_1\eps + b_2\eps^2}, \qquad
\gamma(D) = \frac{\tfrac12 + \sum_{k=1}^{7} e_k \eps^k}{1 + f_1\eps + f_2\eps^2},
\label{eq:formula}
\end{equation}
with the coefficients of Table~\ref{tab:coef}, fitted on the double-precision points $4 \le D \le 30.2$ (so $a_0$ is not
$\Dinf$: it lies $4\times10^{-6}$ above the double-double limit of Sec.~\ref{sec:limit}). It is an empirical fit; no earlier
formula for $\Delta(D)$ was found, and the earlier fit for $\gamma$, $0.466\,(1 - e^{-0.408D})$ on
$3.5 \le D \le 14$~\cite{BlandEtAl2005}, has a different form and limit. They reproduce
$\Delta$ to $5\times10^{-10}$ ($4 \le D \le 4.3$), $4\times10^{-9}$ ($4.3$--$20$) and $10^{-8}$ ($20$--$30$), and $\gamma$ to
$3.4\times10^{-7}$, $3.3\times10^{-8}$ and $2.3\times10^{-9}$, each within the precision of the points. Against the
double-double points, which the fit never saw, the formula holds to $2.7\times10^{-9}$ on $8.25$--$21$,
$7.5\times10^{-9}$ to $D = 30$ and $5.8\times10^{-8}$ to $D = 61$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:delta}). The limit $\gamma_\infty = 1/2$ is
imposed in the $\gamma$ fit, not derived; unconstrained fits of the double-precision $\gamma$ on $D \ge 8$--$12$ give
$0.4996$--$0.4998$. The critical exponent computed in double-double (the root of the determinant of the exact growth-mode
matrix, 28 points for $D = 10$--$61$, accurate to about $10^{-12}$ for $D = 16$--$58$) pins the limit much more tightly: in
free fits in powers of $1/n$, $\lambda_\infty - 2$ ($\lambda = 1/\gamma$) falls by a factor of about 3.7 with each added power,
to $10^{-6}$ at five powers on $D \ge 30$, so $\gamma_\infty = 1/2$ to about $5\times10^{-7}$, with
$\gamma = 1/2 - 0.7581/n + \dots$. The denominators
vanish only at $D = -3.2, -11.0$ ($\Delta$) and $D = -13.3, -20.4$ ($\gamma$). Values are listed in Table~\ref{tab:values}.

\subsection{Comparison with published values}

At $D = 4$ the formula gives $\Delta = 3.4454524$ and $\gamma = 0.3739610$, in agreement with
$\Delta = 3.445452402(3)$~\cite{MartinGarciaGundlach2003} to about $10^{-8}$ and with
$\gamma = 0.373961 \pm 10^{-6}$~\cite{EckerEtAl2026} to about $10^{-7}$. Below $D = 4$ a cubic through the 47-mode
double-precision points at $D = 3.70$--$3.80$ puts the maximum of $\Delta$ at $D = 3.75570$, $\Delta = 3.466774$, in
agreement to $2\times10^{-6}$ with $D = 3.755726$, $\Delta = 3.466772$ of~\cite{EckerEtAl2026}.

At $D = 5$, Ref.~\cite{EckerEtAl2026} gives $\Delta = 3.22176$ and $\gamma = 0.41322$, which are $5.8\times10^{-5}$ and
$9.3\times10^{-5}$ above our values. Our $\Delta(5) = 3.2217019446$ comes from two independent codes: the
double-precision solver at 31, 39 and 47 Fourier modes ($3.221701945 \pm 7\times10^{-11}$ extrapolated) and the
double-double solver at $M = 48$ and $64$ collocation points ($3.22170194461806$, $3.22170194462672$), which agree to
$10^{-11}$. The published data of~\cite{EckerEtAl2026} (repository CriticalSpacetimeCrystal) show their $D = 5$ value
still moving by $-1.66\times10^{-4}$ between 256 and 512 modes, toward ours, in the range $D \gtrsim 4.8$ where they
note that their light-cone expansion order must be increased; their offset from our values grows smoothly from
$2.7\times10^{-7}$ at $D = 4$ (within their stated $\pm10^{-6}$) to $5\times10^{-4}$ at $D = 5.5$, and at $D = 3.7$ the two
agree to $8\times10^{-8}$, so there is no difference of convention. Their $\gamma$ list has no points for
$4.79 < D < 5.49$, where they report that their perturbation method fails, so their $\gamma(5)$ is not a direct
computation in the published data; ours, $0.41312694$ (stable to $10^{-7}$ across resolutions), agrees with Bland and
Kunstatter's $0.4131 \pm 0.0001$~\cite{BlandKunstatter2007}. Below $D = 4$, $\gamma$
extrapolated from our 47-mode points suggests $\gamma(3.5) \lesssim 0.33$, against $0.349 \pm 0.003$
in~\cite{BlandEtAl2005}; our points there are under-resolved, and this is an open check.

\subsection{Physical reading}

Each echo is a factor $e^{\Delta}$ smaller than the last: 31 at $D = 4$, 12 at $D = 10$, 5.2 at $D = \infty$. A
near-critical collapse makes about $\gamma\ln(1/|p-p^*|)/\Delta$ echoes, so each further echo costs
$\Delta/(\gamma\ln10)$ decimal digits of fine-tuning: 4.0 at $D = 4$, 2.4 at $D = 10$ and 1.43 at $D = \infty$. At large
$D$ the field and its gravity weaken: the centre slope of the field falls like $2.72/\sqrt n$ and the departure of $c$ from
1 at the centre like $3.65/n$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:theory}).

\section{The large-$D$ limit}
\label{sec:limit}

\subsection{Is the period analytic in $1/D$?}

Each model in Table~\ref{tab:analytic} is a polynomial in one variable, fitted at 60 digits on the cleaned
double-double points $10 \le D \le 45$ (or $\le 37.5$) and compared with every point above, up to $D = 60.1$, which it
never saw (the data snapshot when the table was made; Fig.~\ref{fig:heldout} uses all points to $D = 61.0$). Every
model fits its range to the level of the points; the unseen points decide. Fitted on $D \le 45$, the $1/D$ series
predicts $\Delta$ fifteen units of $D$ ahead to $5\times10^{-13}$, while the series in half-integer powers miss by 40 to
300 times more, and their limits jump with the degree. Adding one non-analytic term ($D^{-1/2}$, $D^{-3/2}$,
$\ln D/D$, $\ln D/D^2$ or $e^{-D/4}$) to the degree-16 series changes neither the prediction nor the limit, and the
term's coefficient is not determined.

Fitted on all cleaned points, power series in $1/D$ of degree 16--20 and in $1/(D-2)$ of degree 18--20 fit to the

$1.643893752$ (all 823 cleaned points to $D = 61.03$). Over 48 choices of model ($1/D$, $1/(D-2)$), degree, fit range
and cleaning, the limit ranges over $1.643893744$--$1.643893763$ (\texttt{hp/dinf\_budget.py}); fitting on $D \le 45$
alone pins it only to $2.5\times10^{-7}$. Hence, if $\Delta$ is analytic in $1/D$,
\begin{equation}
\Dinf = 1.64389375 \pm 1.5\times10^{-8}, \qquad \omega_\infty = 2\pi/\Dinf = 3.82213589 \pm 4\times10^{-8},
\end{equation}
and $\Delta(D) \approx \Dinf + 10.37/n + \dots$. The $\pm1.5\times10^{-8}$ is a spread over fit choices, so we tested it on
synthetic data with a known limit: the real $D$ grid, noise and junk points like the real ones, and added structures
from a distant pole to a complex pole pair and a logarithmic branch point near the data. In all six cases the 48 fits
put the known limit inside their range, with the centre $0.8$--$0.9\times10^{-8}$ above it, so the bar holds and the
quoted centre is probably high by about $10^{-8}$. The analytic form is the natural default but not proven: as shown in
Sec.~\ref{sec:theory}, the period is selected by an effect the $1/D$ expansion does not see.

\subsection{The conjecture $\Delta \to 0$}

The conjecture $\Delta \to 0$~\cite{EckerEckerGrumiller2026,EckerEtAl2026} comes with no rate. Fitted on
$10 \le D \le 45$ and compared with $45 < D \le 61$: a power-law approach to 0, $D^{-p}$ times a series in $1/D$ with
$p \ge 0.01$, misses the held-out points by $6\times10^{-8}$ or more, against $7\times10^{-13}$ for the analytic series;
a series in $1/\ln D$ with zero limit misses them by $1.3\times10^{-11}$ and still gives $\Delta \approx 0.88$ at
$D = 10^{30}$; adding a $c/\ln D$ term to the analytic series gives $c \approx 4\times10^{-7}$ and leaves the limit at
$1.6438937$. A power-law approach to 0 is excluded; a logarithmic one is disfavoured but cannot be excluded from
$D \le 61$ alone. Bland's hypothesis $\Dinf = 5/2$~\cite{Bland2007} is excluded by $\Delta(14) = 2.29$ and decreasing.

\subsection{Candidate closed forms}

Five constants that a product search placed inside the window of the earlier double-precision estimate (about nine
such coincidences are expected by chance in that search), and four classical constants, were tested. Each was forced as
the limit of a power series in $1/D$ fitted on $10 \le D \le 45$ (degree 14 to 26) and compared with the 351 cleaned
points from 45 to 59.8 (Table~\ref{tab:cand}). All nine are excluded, given analyticity in $1/D$: the closest sits

\section{Large-$D$ theory}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Scaling}

On the double-double solutions at $D = 12$--$60$: $p \approx x F(\theta)/\sqrt n$ near the centre and $p + m$ is
smaller by a further factor of order $1/n$; $c = 1 - O(1/n)$ and $b - 1 = (p^2+m^2)/(n-1)$ to about 3\%; and the
centre series has the exact symmetry $m(x) = p(-x)$. The first harmonic of $F = \sqrt n\,\partial_x p(0)$ falls from
3.480 at $D = 20$ to 2.962 at $D = 60$ and extrapolates to about 2.72; $n(1 - \langle c(0)\rangle)$ and $\langle F^2\rangle$
approach a common limit near 3.7.

\subsection{The $1/n$ expansion never fixes the period}

Write $p = \eps^{1/2}P$, $m = \eps^{1/2}M$, $S = P + M$, $\Delta_P = P - M$, and expand $S = \sum_{k\ge1}\eps^kS_k$,
$\Delta_P = \sum_{k\ge0}\eps^kD_k$, $c = 1 - \sum_{k\ge1}\eps^kG_k$, $b = 1 + \sum_{k\ge2}\eps^kB_k$,
$\omega = \sum_k\eps^k\omega_k$ (integer powers of $\eps$; this is the scaling measured above, and the statements below
hold within this ansatz).

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:1}
Let $F(\theta)$ be any smooth $2\pi$-periodic function with odd harmonics only, $\omega_0, \omega_1, \dots$ any numbers
and $K_1(\theta), K_2(\theta), \dots$ any smooth odd-harmonic functions. Then there is exactly one formal solution with
$D_0 = 2xF$ in which, at every order, $S_k$ is an even polynomial in $x$ starting at $x^2$, $D_k - xK_k$ is an odd
polynomial starting at $x^3$, and $G_k$, $B_{k+1}$ are even polynomials ($B$ starting at $x^2$) with $G_k(1) = 0$. Every
order is regular at the centre and analytic at the light cone.
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof sketch.} Induction on $k$. The $b$ equation gives $B_{k+1}$ algebraically (its coefficient is
$(n-1)b \approx 1/\eps$); the $c$ equation gives $-x\partial_xG_k \in x^2E$ ($E$: even polynomials with periodic
coefficients), so $G_k$ is an even polynomial without $\log x$, fixed by $G_k(1) = 0$; the difference equation gives
$S_k \in x^2E$; and the sum equation reads $x\partial_xD_k - D_k = R_k$ with $R_k$ odd and starting at $x^3$, because
$x\partial_x - 1$ annihilates $xK_j$. The only possible obstruction, an $x^1$ term in $R_k$ that would produce $x\log x$,
never occurs. Parity in $x$ and the harmonic content in $\theta$ are preserved at each step. Polynomials are analytic
at $x = 1$, and nothing constrains $F$ or the $\omega_k$. $\square$

The first orders (sympy, to fourth order) are $P_0 = xF$, $B_2 = 2x^2F^2$, $G_1 = F^2(1-x^2)$,
$S_1 = 2x^2(F + \omega_0F')$ and $D_1 = x^3(\omega_0^2F'' + 3\omega_0F' + 2F - 2F^3) + xK_1$. Substituting the expansion
through $S_3$, $G_3$, $B_4$ into the exact equations with $F$ from the $D = 60$ solution, the relative residuals fall
like $n^{-3}$ for $n = 50$--$800$, both for $\omega_0 = 2\pi/1.64389$ and for a wrong $\omega_0 = 2\pi/1.4$.

So if $\Delta(D)$ is analytic in $1/D$, its expansion coefficients cannot be computed order by order from the field
equations: the selection works through terms smaller than any power of $1/n$. The same holds for the critical
exponent: the expansion of growing perturbations $e^{\lambda\tau}$ goes through for any $\lambda$ (the same induction
with $\partial_\tau \to \lambda + \omega\partial_\theta$; checked with sympy through order 2), so $\gamma = 1/\lambda$ is also
selected beyond all orders.

\emph{Why the ansatz.} The scaling is forced by one measured fact, $c - 1 = O(1/n)$ (gravity weak;
$n(1 - \langle c(0)\rangle) \to 3.65$): the $b$ equation gives $b - 1 \approx (p^2+m^2)/(n-1)$, the $c$ equation then gives
$c - 1 = O(n(b-1)) = O(p^2)$, so $c - 1 = O(1/n)$ requires $p = O(n^{-1/2})$; and the $O(n)$ terms of the $p$, $m$ equations
force $p + m = O(p/n)$, the measured $p + m = O(n^{-3/2})$. Integer powers of $\eps$ beyond the overall $\eps^{1/2}$ are an
assumption (analyticity in $1/n$), consistent with the held-out tests on $\Delta(D)$ but not implied by them.

\subsection{The leading-order problem}

At leading order ($c = b = 1$) the equations describe a free wave in self-similar form. For one harmonic
$e^{i\mu\theta}$ put $s = i\mu\omega$, $\alpha = (1+s)/2$, $\beta = (2+s)/2$, $\kappa = (n+1)/2$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:2}
The solution regular at the centre is, up to a constant, $p - m = x\,{}_2F_1(\alpha,\beta;\kappa;x^2)$. Near the light
cone it contains the non-analytic branch $(1-x^2)^{n/2-1-s}$ with coefficient
\begin{equation}
C(n,s) = \frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\!\left(s+1-\frac n2\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1+s}{2}\right)\Gamma\!\left(\frac{2+s}{2}\right)},
\label{eq:C}
\end{equation}
which is finite and non-zero for every real $\omega \ne 0$, odd $\mu$ and $n \ge 2$. Hence no time-periodic
self-similar solution of the linear problem is analytic at the light cone, for any $D \ge 4$.
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof sketch.} Eliminating $p+m$ gives Gauss's equation for $y = x\,w(x^2)$; regularity (for $n = 2$, parity)
selects ${}_2F_1(\alpha,\beta;\kappa;z)$, and the connection formula~\cite{AbramowitzStegun} applies because
$\kappa - \alpha - \beta = n/2 - 1 - s$ is not an integer; its second coefficient is $C(n,s)$, a ratio of Gamma functions
whose arguments have non-zero imaginary parts. $\square$

For the fundamental at $\omega = \omega_\infty$, $|C| = 0.0121$, 0.0114, 0.0124, 0.0192, 0.0405 at $n = 18$, 38, 58, 200,
1000. The free wave equation in similarity coordinates was analysed in 3+1 dimensions by Donninger~\cite{Donninger2008};
the leading-order freedom of the large-$D$ problem is also noted in~\cite{EckerEckerGrumiller2026}.

\subsection{What gravity does to the non-analytic piece}

The non-analytic piece is a fast WKB mode; in Proposition~\ref{prop:2} it is $x^{2-n}(1-x^2)^{n/2-1-s}$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:3}
Let $c = 1 - \eps F^2(1-x^2) + O(\eps^2)$ as in Proposition~\ref{prop:1}, and
$\Phi(x) = \ln x - \tfrac12\ln(1-x^2)$. Perturbations
$\delta p = e^{-n\Phi}(A + O(1/n))$, $\delta m = e^{-n\Phi}\big(\tfrac{1-x}{1+x}A + O(1/n)\big)$ solve the linearized
equations on $0 < x < 1$ to this order if and only if
\begin{equation}
x(1-x^2)\,\partial_xA - 2\omega x^2\,\partial_\theta A = (1 + x - 2x^2F^2)\,A ,
\end{equation}
i.e.\ along the characteristics $\theta = \theta_0 + \omega\ln(1-x^2)$,
$A = A_0(\theta_0)\,\frac{x}{1-x}\exp\!\big(-\int_0^x \frac{2tF(\theta_0 + \omega\ln(1-t^2))^2}{1-t^2}\,dt\big)$.
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof sketch.} The order-$n$ terms give $x(1-x^2)\Phi' = 1$ and $\delta m/\delta p = (1-x)/(1+x)$; at order 1 the
corrections satisfy a singular linear system whose left null vector $(1+x, -(1-x))$ gives the transport equation as the
solvability condition. Perturbations of $b$, $c$ and $G\,\delta p$ enter one order later (confirmed symbolically from the
exact equations). $\square$

Integrating the linearized equations directly from $x = 0.9$ to $0.72$ ($F = 2.72\sin\theta + 0.22\sin3\theta$,
$\omega = \omega_\infty$) reproduces the transported $A$ with relative errors $0.57$, $0.26$, $0.13$, $0.062$, $0.031$ at
$n = 100$--$1600$, i.e.\ like $1/n$; with the full $O(1/n)$ background the numbers are nearly the same. Three
consequences: (i) gravity acts on the non-analytic piece at order 1 (over this interval it multiplies it by about 40),
and shifts the light-cone exponent by $\langle F^2\rangle \approx 3.7$, so an expansion in the wave amplitude is not a route
to the selection; (ii) the factor never vanishes, so gravity cannot cancel the piece on the way to the light cone;
(iii) every characteristic reaches $x = 0$, where the fast mode is the singular branch $x^{2-n}$, so a regular solution
carries no fast mode on $0 < x < 1$ at any order of this WKB expansion. The non-analytic piece must come from a Stokes
phenomenon.

\subsection{The light-cone coefficient as a Stokes multiplier}

A divergent series records the exponentially small terms it switches on in its late orders~\cite{Dingle1973}. At
leading order this can be made exact, because the $1/n$ expansion of $x\,{}_2F_1$ from Proposition~\ref{prop:2} is
explicit.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:4}
Let $0 < x < 1$, $\Phi = \ln x - \tfrac12\ln(1-x^2)$, $\tau_\ell = \Phi - i\pi(\ell + \tfrac12)$, and let
$y = x\,{}_2F_1(\alpha,\beta;\kappa;x^2)$ with $\kappa = (n+1)/2$, $y \sim \sum_k y_k\eps^k$, $\eps = 1/n$. If
$|\Phi| < (\sqrt3/2)\pi$ (equivalently $0.066 < x < 0.998$), then
\begin{equation}
y_k = K\,x^2(1-x^2)^{-1-s}\,\frac{\Gamma(k+s+\frac12)}{\tau_0^{\,k+s+\frac12}}\,\big(1 + O(1/k)\big), \qquad
K = e^{-i\pi(1+s)}\,\frac{2^{-s-1/2}}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)} .
\label{eq:prop4}
\end{equation}
The $\ell = -1$ singularity contributes at the same rate but with a $k$-independent relative weight of $8\times10^{-5}$
($x = 0.5$) to $5\times10^{-8}$ ($x = 0.95$). If $|\Phi| > (\sqrt3/2)\pi$ the late orders are governed instead by the
$x$-independent singularities $\pm i\pi$.
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof (condensed).} (i) \emph{Euler integral.} For $n > 1$,
${}_2F_1 = \frac{\Gamma(\kappa)}{\Gamma(\beta)\Gamma(\kappa-\beta)}\int_0^1 t^{\beta-1}(1-t)^{\kappa-\beta-1}(1-zt)^{-\alpha}dt$; with
$1 - t = e^{-2\tau}$, $y = x\,P(\kappa)\cdot2\int_0^\infty e^{-\tau/\eps}G(\tau)\,d\tau$, where
$G = e^{(2\beta-1)\tau}(1-e^{-2\tau})^{\beta-1}(1-z+ze^{-2\tau})^{-\alpha}$ and $P = \Gamma(\kappa)/(\Gamma(\beta)\Gamma(\kappa-\beta))$.
(ii) \emph{Watson's lemma.} Write $G = \tau^{\beta-1}H$; $H$ is analytic except at the simple zeros $\tau = \tau_\ell$ of
$1 - z + ze^{-2\tau}$, where $H \propto (1-\tau/\tau_\ell)^{-\alpha}$, and at the branch points $\tau = i\pi m$, $m \ne 0$. On
$\tau > 0$, $|G| \le Ce^{\tau}$, so $2\int_0^\infty e^{-\tau/\eps}G \sim 2\sum_m H_m\Gamma(m+\beta)\eps^{m+\beta}$.
(iii) \emph{Darboux.} The radius of convergence of $\sum H_m\tau^m$ is $\min(|\tau_0|, \pi)$; if $|\tau_0| < \pi$ the only singularities on
that circle are $\tau_0$ and $\tau_{-1}$, giving $H_m = \sum_{\ell=0,-1}h_\ell\,\frac{\Gamma(m+\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\,m!}\,\tau_\ell^{-m}(1+O(1/m))$.
(iv) \emph{Prefactor.} $P(\kappa)(2\eps)^\beta(1+\eps)^{-\beta}\Gamma(\beta) = 1 + \sum_j p_j\eps^j$, from Binet's expansion of
$\ln\Gamma(\kappa) - \ln\Gamma(\kappa-\beta)$, whose Borel transform is singular only at $\tau = i\pi m$, a larger radius.
(v) \emph{Product lemma.} If $A = \sum a_k\eps^k$ has Borel radius $\rho_A$ and $B = \sum b_j\eps^j$ has $\rho_B > \rho_A$, then
$(AB)_k = b_0a_k(1+O(1/k))$; hence $y_k = \frac{x\,2^{1-\beta}}{\Gamma(\beta)}\Gamma(k+\beta)H_k(1+O(1/k))$.
(vi) \emph{Constant.} $\Gamma(k+\alpha)\Gamma(k+\beta)/\Gamma(k+1) = \Gamma(k+s+\frac12)(1+O(1/k))$, and evaluating $h_0$ in closed
form (with $e^{2\tau_0} = z/(z-1)$) gives the prefactor $x^2(1-x^2)^{-1-s}$ times $K$. If $|\tau_0| > \pi$ the radius is $\pi$
and step (iii) applies to $\pm i\pi$ instead. $\square$

Numerically, the late orders computed to order 122 at 90 digits agree: at $x = 0.9$, $y_k$/prediction $- 1$ extrapolates to
$(-0.9 - 2.3i)\times10^{-3}$ over $k = 100$--$122$; the derived constant equals $K$ to $1 + 3.65/k$ at $x = 0.5$, 0.8 and 0.9;
and at $x = 0.999$ and $0.9999$ the measured singulant is $-3.135i \to -i\pi$, not $\Phi - i\pi/2$, as the statement predicts.
The singularities $\pm i\pi$ come from the Gamma-function prefactor and the endpoint factor $(1-e^{-2\tau})^{\beta-1}$; their
exponentials $e^{\mp i\pi n}$ are pure phases, not exponentially small terms, so they do not affect the Stokes-line
argument, which concerns the $\tau_\ell$.

Hence, with $\chi = \tau_0 = \Phi - i\pi/2$: $e^{-n\chi} = e^{in\pi/2}x^{-n}(1-x^2)^{n/2}$ is the fast mode of Proposition~\ref{prop:3}; $\chi$ vanishes only
at $x = \infty$, so the mode is born at the outer singular point; its Stokes line is the real axis $x > 1$ approached
from above, where $\chi = \ln(x/\sqrt{x^2-1})$; on $0 < x < 1$ nothing is switched on (the optimally truncated series
matches the exact solution to $4\times10^{-40}$ at $x = 0.9$, $n = 60$, against a singular-branch size of
$7\times10^{-21}$). The Stokes line ends at the light cone, and $C(n,s)$ is the Stokes multiplier.

\subsection{The inner problem}
\label{sec:inner}

At $x \sim \sqrt n$ the expansion is not uniform: gravity is of order one and fast and slow modes are not separated.
In physical variables this is the region $r = O(\sqrt n)$ at fixed $t$, where the field equations at leading order are, up
to a change of variables, the large-$D$ system of Rozali and Way~\cite{RozaliWay2018} (see Proposition~\ref{prop:5}). With $\xi = x/\sqrt n$, $y = p - m$, $\sigma = \sqrt n\,(p+m)$, $b - 1 = \eta/n$ and
$L = \xi^2\partial_\xi + \omega\xi\partial_\theta$, the equations become, at leading order,
\begin{equation}
L\,y = c\,\sigma, \qquad L\,\sigma = c\,\xi\,\partial_\xi y + c\,(\tfrac12y^2 - 1)\,y, \qquad
\xi\,\partial_\xi c = \tfrac12\,c\,y^2, \qquad \eta = \tfrac12y^2,
\end{equation}
with no large parameter left. Its formal solution regular at $\xi = 0$ reproduces the top-degree coefficients of the
outer polynomials of Proposition~\ref{prop:1} (checked to $2\times10^{-13}$ at orders 1--10). Without gravity, with
$t = 1/\xi$ and $w = t^{-s}y$ along characteristics, it reduces to Hermite's (Weber's) equation; the regular solution is
$y = 2\xi\,{}_2F_0(\alpha,\beta;;2\xi^2)$, whose Stokes constant gives, by the duplication formula,
\begin{equation}
C(n,s) \approx -\,i\,\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\Gamma(1+s)}\,(n+1)^{s+1/2}\,e^{-i\pi\Lambda}\,\big(1 + O(s^2/n)\big), \qquad \Lambda = \tfrac n2 - 1 - s,
\end{equation}
Weber's classical Stokes constant~\cite{DLMF}; the ratio to the exact~\eqref{eq:C} is $1.00014$ at $n = 10^5$ ($\mu = 1$).

In the inner region the fast mode obeys $\xi\partial_\xi a + 2\omega\partial_\theta a = 2F^2a$ (the large-$x$ limit of
Proposition~\ref{prop:3}), so the fast-type Stokes multiplier is a function of the characteristic label,
$S(\theta_0) = \sum_\nu S_\nu e^{i\nu\theta_0}$ with $\theta_0 = \theta - 2\omega\ln\xi$; the Borel singularity stays at
$\zeta = \tfrac12$ and the exponents shift by $\langle F^2\rangle$. The selection rule is stated as a conjecture.

\begin{conjecture}[selection at $D = \infty$]\label{conj}
For $n = D - 2$ let $F_n(\theta) = \sqrt n\,\partial_xp(0,\theta)$ and $\omega_n = 2\pi/\Delta(D)$ be the profile and frequency
of the critical solution. Assume (H1) $F_n \to F_\infty$ uniformly on a strip $|\mathrm{Im}\,\theta| < \sigma$ for some
$\sigma > 0$, and $\omega_n \to \omega_\infty > 0$ (the data give $\sigma \approx 1.4$, the harmonic decay rate); and (H2) the
formal family of Proposition~\ref{prop:1} with data $(F_n, \omega_n, K_k)$ is the asymptotic expansion of the solution
uniformly on $\delta \le x \le 1 - \delta$ (checked: residuals $\propto n^{-3}$ through third order). Then
$S_\nu(F_\infty, \omega_\infty) = 0$ for every odd $\nu$. Conversely, a zero of $S$ at which the Jacobian with respect to
$(F, \omega)$, modulo the phase, is invertible should continue to critical solutions for all large $n$.
\end{conjecture}

\emph{Argument.} The inner problem resums the outer series' top-degree terms; the outer series' light-cone exponential is
fixed by the inner singularity at $x = \infty$ (exact in the linear case, where the outer late-order constant of
Proposition~\ref{prop:4} is the inner Weber Stokes constant); and analyticity at the light cone forces $C = 0$. The map
$S \mapsto C$ is, at leading order in $1/n$, a composition of three invertible maps: (1) inner--outer matching on
$1 \ll x \ll \sqrt n$, where the inner fast mode $e^{-1/(2\xi^2)}\xi^{2\langle F^2\rangle}e^{\Pi(\theta)}S(\theta_0)$, with
$\Pi = \omega^{-1}\!\int(F^2 - \langle F^2\rangle)$, matches the outer WKB mode $e^{-n\Phi}A$ on the branch above the real axis
$x > 1$; (2) the outer transport of Proposition~\ref{prop:3} along $x > 1$ with real $\theta$, which has real coefficients
while $\eps x^2F^2 \ll 1$ and acts along real characteristics as multiplication by a non-vanishing function composed with
a shift; and (3) the half-monodromy at the regular singular point $x = 1$, which continues the singular Floquet branch
$(1-x)^\Lambda\varphi(\theta - \omega'\ln(1-x))$, $\omega' = \omega/(1 - \langle G(1)\rangle)$, from $x > 1$ to $x < 1$ and multiplies
harmonic $\mu$ by $e^{-i\pi\Lambda}e^{\pm\pi\mu\omega'}$. Hence $C \equiv 0 \Leftrightarrow S \equiv 0$ at leading order; in the
linear case this is exact.

Rigour fails at three points (a staged proof roadmap, with the selection theorem for an $M$-harmonic model
written out lemma by lemma, is in the repository's PROOF.md). (a) Borel summability of the nonlinear series is not proved; the outer nonlinear late
orders also carry $x$-independent singularities of modulus about 0.13, driven by high harmonics, and one must show that
these give only ambiguities in the free data, not light-cone branches (in the linear case the analogous $\pm i\pi$ are
harmless pure phases, which is suggestive but not a proof). (b) Uniformity in the harmonic index (narrower than it looks: stated without harmonics the condition uses $F$ only on the
real line, and the weights below cancel in every tuning equation, so a tail lemma is needed only for the jump-conditioned
truncation, whose tail beyond $M = 7$ is estimated at a tenth of what a $10^{-3}$ certificate tolerates): the weights
$e^{\pi|\mu|\omega/2}$ grow like $e^{6.0|\mu|}$ while $F$'s harmonics decay only like $e^{-1.4|\mu|}$, so an $O(1/n)$ error in a
high harmonic is amplified when it mixes into low harmonics through $e^{\Pi}$; a proof needs a function-space formulation,
e.g.\ on the strip $|\mathrm{Im}\,\theta| < \sigma$ with weights in a dual norm. (c) Uniformity at $x = 1$: the outer WKB
amplitude is singular there ($A \propto (1-x)^{-1}$) and must be matched to the Frobenius solution on
$1/n \ll |1-x| \ll 1$; the exponents agree ($\Lambda = n/2 - 1 - s + \langle F^2\rangle + O(1/n)$ on both sides), but the matching
has not been written out.

\emph{Computations with gravity.} Two independent implementations (lateral solutions on either side of the Stokes
line, and Borel--Darboux fits in ball arithmetic) reproduce the linear constant to $10^{-11}$ and $6\times10^{-27}$. With
$F = A\sin\theta$ alone, both find an isolated zero of the multiplier at $A = 2.542148$, $\omega = 2.969777$ (a computer-assisted
proof in ball arithmetic, following the template of Baldom\'a et al.~\cite{BaldomaEtAl2023} for inner Stokes constants,
with a validated Lohner integrator and Krawczyk's test, certifies that one component of
the jump at one radius has exactly one zero, a simple one, within $10^{-9}$ of
$(A, \omega/\omega_\infty) = (2.5421480475, 0.7769940136)$; that this component vanishing forces the whole jump to vanish is
proved, and so is the analyticity near $\xi = 0$ that identifies the computed solution with the Borel sum, which leaves
one lemma, analyticity on an annulus $0.0624 \le |\xi| \le 0.2$, for the full one-harmonic condition; an
independent C++ implementation with the CAPD library confirms it and certifies a box ten times larger;
repository, proof/A\_certify.md and A\_review.md; Reiterer and Trubowitz~\cite{ReitererTrubowitz2019} gave a
computer-assisted existence proof of the $D = 4$ critical solution itself); it is not the
physical solution ($\omega$ 22\% low) and does not survive adding harmonic 3. Truncating harmonics is not a controlled
approximation here: the linear multipliers grow like $F_\nu e^{\pi|\nu|\omega/2}$.

\emph{Three harmonics.} With $F = A\sin\theta + (F_3e^{3i\theta} + \mathrm{c.c.})$ the joint solve of $S_1 = S_3 = 0$ for
$(A, \omega, F_3)$ is badly conditioned (Jacobian singular values $6\times10^9$, $5\times10^9$, $23$, $0.4$): the de-mixed
harmonic-1 residual picks up about $10^4$ times the harmonic-3 one ($\sim e^{\pi\omega}$ times the finite-$\xi$ de-mixing
error), and the joint solve stops on false near-zeros where a small $S_3$ cancels $S_1$. A nested solve removes this:
at fixed $(A, \omega)$ solve $S_3 = 0$ for $F_3$ to $10^{-11}$, then apply Newton's method to $(A, \omega)$ for $S_1 = 0$ (a $2\times2$ problem of
condition about 2). It has one zero, the same from two starts and at three matching radii (moving by less than
$10^{-7}$): $A = 2.7265$, $\omega = 1.0156\,\omega_\infty$ ($\Delta = 1.6186$), $F_3/F_1 = 0.0530 + 0.0041i$,
$\langle F^2\rangle = 3.727$. The shape is that of the $D = \infty$ solution extrapolated from the data
($A \approx 2.70$--$2.72$, $|F_3/F_1| \approx 0.050$--$0.058$, $\langle F^2\rangle \approx 3.65$--$3.7$), and $\omega$ is 1.56\%
above $\omega_\infty$, against 22\% below with one harmonic.

\emph{Five harmonics.} At $M = 5$ the couplings grow further ($\partial r_3/\partial B_5 \approx 10^{13}$,
$\partial r_1/\partial B_5 \approx 10^{17}$) and the nested solve's homotopy stalls. A hierarchical solve converges: $S_5 = 0$
innermost, then $S_3 = 0$, then $S_1 = 0$ for $(A, \omega)$, each level a damped $2\times2$ Newton step judged by step size, with
$S_5$ first solved at a small radius where its jump is linear and then polished at the matching radius. At fixed
$(2.72, \omega_\infty)$ it gives $F_3/F_1 = 0.05486 + 0.00541i$ and $F_5/F_1 = 0.00309 + 0.00054i$, against the amplitude
expansion's $0.05482 + 0.00543i$ and $0.00310 + 0.00056i$ (two methods sharing no code; the differences are of the size of
the omitted $F_7$). The full selection, iterated to its noise floor, gives $A = 2.71858$, $\omega = 0.99923\,\omega_\infty$
($\Delta = 1.64516$, $0.077\%$ above $\Dinf$; steady to $8\times10^{-6}$ in $\omega/\omega_\infty$ over the last steps). The offset in
$\omega$ thus goes $-22\%$, $+1.56\%$, $-0.077\%$ for $M = 1$, $3$, $5$, alternating in sign and shrinking fifteen- to twentyfold
per added pair of harmonics; Aitken's extrapolation of the three gives $\omega/\omega_\infty = 1.0003$.

\emph{Amplitude expansion.} Writing $F = A\sin\theta + \sum_{\nu\ge3} f_\nu(A)e^{i\nu\theta}$ (plus c.c.), harmonic $\mu$
first appears at order $A^{|\mu|}$, so every harmonic is kept exactly. Tuning $f_\nu$ order by order so that the
multipliers of all harmonics $|\nu| \ge 3$ vanish, at $A = 2.72$ and $\omega = \omega_\infty$, gives
$|F_3/F_1| = 0.0551$ and $|F_5/F_1| = 0.00314$, with ratios $f_{\nu+2}/f_\nu \approx qA^2$, $|q|A^2 \approx 0.063$, nearly
independent of $\nu$. The double-double solutions at $D = 20$--$60$, extrapolated in $1/n$ (degree 2--3), give
$|F_3/F_1| \to 0.0552$--$0.0549$ and $|F_5/F_1| \to 0.0034$--$0.0032$, and the ratios $|F_{\nu+2}/F_\nu|$
($\nu = 1,\dots,9$) extrapolate to $0.055$--$0.072$, a finite decay rate of 1.3--1.45 per harmonic, as predicted
(Fig.~\ref{fig:spectrum}; the extrapolations are not fully converged). This is the first number the inner problem has
produced that can be checked against the solutions, and it agrees. The same relation evaluated at each $D$'s own
amplitude and $\omega$ (a heuristic) tracks the measured $F_3/F_1$ at $D = 20$--$60$ to within $0.004$--$0.006$ (0.126, 0.099,
0.087, 0.080, 0.075 against 0.130, 0.105, 0.093, 0.085, 0.080).

\emph{The harmonic-1 condition.} With the higher harmonics tuned,
$S_1/S_1^{(1)} = 1 + \sigma_1A^2 + \sigma_2A^4 + \dots$. The late orders of harmonic $\nu$ at order $k$ contain every neighbour
family $\nu'$ with $|\nu'| \le k$ (they enter through the label $\theta_0$ at relative order $1/j$), and the fits must include
them all; a basis with only $\nu' - \nu = \pm2, \pm4, \pm6$ is good through $\sigma_4$ but fails from $\sigma_6$ on. With all
families, in ball arithmetic at 640 bits, the values at $\omega_\infty$ agree between $J = 750$ and $1000$ terms to about
$10^{-19}$: $\sigma_1 = 0.74358457992 + 0.52447485276i$, $\sigma_2 = 0.11879383784 + 0.39807457035i$,
$\sigma_3 = -0.05397185243 + 0.11658742432i$, $\sigma_4 = -0.02886475753 + 0.01077835879i$,
$\sigma_5 = -0.00555413676 - 0.00280595708i$, $\sigma_6 = (-2.7853745790 - 10.776628405i)\times10^{-4}$,
$\sigma_7 = (1.0223846436 - 1.4905053530i)\times10^{-4}$.

At $\omega_\infty$ the coefficients $\tau_k$ of $\ln S_1$ in $x = A^2$ have $\arg\tau_k/\pi = 0.878$, $0.944$, $0.978$, $0.986$,
$0.992$, $0.995$ for $k = 2$--$7$, approaching $\pi$, as a real zero $x_0$ requires ($\tau_k \to -1/(k x_0^k)$; a complex $x_0$
would turn them with $k$). Pad\'e approximants $[1/5]$ to $[5/1]$ of $(\ln S_1)'$ put the zero at $A_0 = 2.728$--$2.756$ with
$\mathrm{Im}\,A_0 = -0.013$ to $-0.027$ (residues 1.05--1.22), and ratio estimates from the last coefficients give
$2.744 - 0.026i$ and $2.748 - 0.013i$. The amplitude extrapolated from the solutions is 2.70--2.72, so at $\omega_\infty$ the
harmonic-1 condition has a nearly real zero within about 1\% of it.

Across $\omega$ two families of estimates of where the zero is exactly real approach $\omega_\infty$ from opposite sides
(Table~\ref{tab:bracket}, Fig.~\ref{fig:selection}; $\sigma_1$--$\sigma_{12}$ at $\omega/\omega_\infty = 0.99$, $1.00$, $1.01$, $\sigma_1$--$\sigma_8$ at five values
$0.98$--$1.02$, $\sigma_1$--$\sigma_7$ at seven values $0.97$--$1.03$). The crossings $\arg\tau_k = \pi$ assume a simple zero,
$\tau_k \to -1/(k x_0^k)$. The coefficients behave as $-c/(k x_0^k)$ with an effective exponent $c$ that falls toward 1 as
orders are added (last column), so $x_0$ is a simple zero, the departure of $c$ from 1 is contamination by singularities
beyond it, and the crossings from above are the asymptotically correct estimator. Their distance from the limit shrinks by a
factor of about 0.4--0.6 per order, to $1.00011$ at $k = 12$ and still falling, and Aitken's extrapolations of consecutive
triples scatter around 1 at the $10^{-4}$ level. The ratio estimates, which let $c$ float, come up from below more slowly; Pad\'e approximants
of $(\ln S_1)'$ put $\mathrm{Im}\,A_0 = 0$ at $0.981$--$0.991$ with seven coefficients, $0.987$--$0.998$ with eight, $0.992$--$0.998$ with
nine, and with ten to twelve they straddle $\omega_\infty$ (with twelve, the five central approximants agree at
$0.99987$--$0.99991$, and at $\omega_\infty$ they are real to $|\mathrm{Im}\,A_0| \approx 1.4\times10^{-4}$, at $A_0 = 2.7196$). At
twelve orders the bracket is $0.99915$--$1.00011$. A third estimator agrees: the ratios $\sigma_k/\sigma_{k+1}$ grow linearly
in $k$, so $S_1$ is numerically an entire function of $A^2$ of exponential type, and the series of $e^{-\bar\kappa A^2}S_1$ (same
zeros, no cancellation) puts the real zero at $\omega/\omega_\infty = 0.99988$ at twelve orders, with $A_0 = 2.7194$ isolated from
the other zeros. The five-harmonic truncation ($0.999228$) lies inside the bracket. The selection rule thus picks $\omega/\omega_\infty = 0.9999$--$1.0001$ at amplitude
$A_0 = 2.720$ (the solutions: 2.70--2.72): $\Dinf = 1.6439 \pm 0.0002$ from the $D = \infty$ inner equations alone, with no
input from the finite-$D$ solutions, against $1.64389375$ from them. Whether the remaining $10^{-4}$ (the Pad\'e value lies
$1.1\times10^{-4}$ below 1) closes needs more orders.

\emph{The critical exponent.} The double-double data give $\lambda_\infty = 2$ to about $10^{-6}$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}),
and the inner problem explains it. Proposition~\ref{prop:1} holds for perturbations as well, so $\lambda$, like $\omega$, is
selected beyond all orders; but one growth rate of the inner problem is exact. In physical inner variables, $\rho = r/\sqrt n$
and $t$, one has $\xi = \rho/(-t)$, $L = \rho\,\partial_t$ at fixed $\rho$ and $\xi\partial_\xi = \rho\partial_\rho$ at fixed $t$. Put
$s = \rho^2/2 = r^2/(2n)$, $y = \sqrt s\,Y$ and $\sigma = \sqrt2\,s\,S$.

\begin{proposition}[an exact unstable mode at $D = \infty$]\label{prop:5}
(i) In these variables the inner equations are the nonlinear heat equation (Rozali and Way's leading-order system,
below)
\begin{equation}
Y_t = cS,\qquad S_t = c\,\big(Y_s + \tfrac14 Y^3\big),\qquad c_s = \tfrac14 c\,Y^2 ,
\end{equation}
with no explicit $s$ or $t$; they are invariant under $s$-translation, $t$-translation, the scaling
$(t, s, Y, S) \to (at, a^2s, Y/a, S/a^2)$ and the reparametrizations $t \to T(t)$, $c \to T'c$.
(ii) Let $(y, \sigma, c)(\xi, \theta)$ be a DSS inner solution with frequency $\omega$, regular at $\xi = 0$, with $y = 2F\xi + O(\xi^3)$
and $c = 1 + F^2\xi^2 + O(\xi^4)$. Let $P$ be the periodic solution of $P + \omega P' = -2F^2$ and $X = \xi\partial_\xi + \omega\partial_\theta$. Then
\begin{equation}
\delta y = e^{2\tau}\Big(\frac{\xi y_\xi - y}{\xi^2} + P\,Xy\Big),\quad
\delta\sigma = e^{2\tau}\Big(\frac{\xi\sigma_\xi - 2\sigma}{\xi^2} + P\,X\sigma\Big),\quad
\delta c = e^{2\tau}\Big(\frac{c_\xi}{\xi} + (P + \omega P')\,c + P\,Xc\Big)
\end{equation}
solves the linearized inner equations: an exact perturbation with $\lambda = 2$. It is regular at $\xi = 0$, keeps $c(t, 0) = 1$,
and has leading profile $\delta y = 2f\xi e^{2\tau} + O(\xi^3)$, $f = (2 + \omega\partial_\theta)(F + \omega F') - 2F^3 + P\,(F + \omega F')$.
(iii) Wherever the fast-type Stokes multipliers of the background are defined by Borel summation, those of this
perturbation are a linear image of them and vanish with them; so under Conjecture~\ref{conj}, $\lambda = 2$ is an exact growth
rate of the $D = \infty$ critical solution.
\end{proposition}

\emph{Proof sketch.} (i) Direct substitution (sympy, residual $0$); no coefficient depends on $s$ or $t$. This system is
Rozali and Way's leading-order flat-space equations~\cite{RozaliWay2018}: with their $r$, $\tau = \sqrt D\,t$ and lapse factor
$1 - \delta_0$ identified with our $\rho$, $t$ and $c^2$, their equations become $\phi_s = c^{-1}\partial_t(c^{-1}\phi_t)$ and
$c_s = \phi_t^2/c$, and $Y = 2\phi_t/c$, $S = Y_t/c$ then satisfy the three equations above identically (sympy). They
noted its parabolic character with space and time swapped; what is new here is the symmetry argument for the DSS
solution. (ii) With
$\tau = -\ln(-t)$, $\partial_t = e^\tau X$ on a DSS background and $\partial_s = e^{2\tau}\xi^{-2}\,\xi\partial_\xi$ at fixed $t$, so a generator of
scaling weight $k$ gives a perturbation $\propto e^{k\tau}$; $s$-translation has weight $2$. It changes $c$ at the centre by
$2F^2e^{2\tau}$, which the reparametrization $t \to t - e^\tau P(\theta)$ cancels exactly when $P + \omega P' = -2F^2$, solvable
harmonic by harmonic since $1 + i\nu\omega \ne 0$. With $y = \sum_j d_j\xi^{2j+1}$, the $s$-translation part is
$\sum_k 2(k+1)d_{k+1}\xi^{2k+1}$, and $X$ preserves parity. (iii) $\delta Z = \mathcal D Z$ for a first-order operator $\mathcal D$ with
coefficients analytic away from $\xi = 0$; the regular formal solution with given leading profile is unique, and lateral
Borel sums commute with $\mathcal D$, so the jump of $\delta Z$ is $\mathcal D$ applied to the background's jump. $\square$

As a check, the perturbation recursion (gravity included, $\delta c(0) = 0$ imposed, nothing about symmetries built in) run at
$\lambda = 2$ from $f$ reproduces the predicted coefficients to $10^{-180}$ in every resolved harmonic, while $\lambda = 1.9$ or $2.1$
miss by $7.5\%$ at the third order. The mode is physical, not gauge: the reparametrization is gauge and $t$-translation
($\lambda = 1$) shifts the accumulation time, but $s$-translation shifts $r^2$, with $r$ the areal radius. So
$r_{\rm BH}^2 \propto p - p^*$ and $\gamma_\infty = 1/2$, the DSS counterpart of Rozali and Way's estimate~\cite{RozaliWay2018}, exact at
$D = \infty$ given the conditions stated next:
\emph{if Conjecture~\ref{conj} holds, the $D = \infty$ solution has no unstable mode other than $\lambda = 2$ and the gauge mode
$\lambda = 1$, and $\lambda(D)$ is continuous at $1/n = 0$, then $\gamma_\infty = 1/2$.} At finite $D$ the outer region and the $1/n$
corrections depend on $r$ explicitly, so the mode survives as an ordinary unstable mode with $\lambda = 2 + O(1/n)$; the data
give $2 + 3.0324/n$. Open are the uniqueness of the unstable mode at $D = \infty$ and the coefficient $3.0324$, which needs
the $O(1/n)$ inner equations.

\subsection{Relation to the next-to-leading-order condition of Ref.~\cite{EckerEckerGrumiller2026}}
\label{sec:eeg}

Ecker, Ecker and Grumiller use the same gauge: their $f = \alpha/a$ is our $c$, with $f = 1$ at the self-similarity horizon
$x = 1$, and their profile is $\beta(\tau) \approx \sqrt{2/n}\,F(\omega\tau)$. At next-to-leading order they require
$f \le 1$ near the horizon at the zeros of $\beta$, which gives their Eq.~(20); in our variables,
$\omega^2F'' + 3\omega F' = 0$ at the zeros of $F$. Within our framework this condition does not select $\Delta$, for the
following reasons. The inequality $f \le 1$ holds for every regular solution, whatever the period: from
$xb_x = b(p^2+m^2) - (n-1)b(b-1)$ with $b(0) = 1$, $b$ can never drop below 1, so $xc_x = (n-1)c(b-1) \ge 0$ and
$c \le c(1) = 1$. In the formal family of Proposition~\ref{prop:1}, near a zero of $F$,
\begin{equation}
b - 1 = 2\eps^2x^2F(\theta - \delta\theta)^2 + 2\eps^4\omega^2x^4F'^2 + \dots, \qquad
\delta\theta(x) = -\eps\omega x^2\,\frac{3F' + \omega F''}{2F'},
\end{equation}
so the next-to-leading-order term is the cross term of a square whose square term arrives one order later; the
discriminant $16\omega^2x^6F'^4$ is positive for every $F$ and $\omega$. The double-double solutions are consistent with
this: they violate Eq.~(20) [$(\omega^2F''+3\omega F')/(3\omega F') = 0.98$--$1.06$ at $D = 12$--$60$, since $F$ is nearly
antisymmetric about its zeros], the zero line of $p - m$ tilts as $\delta\theta(x)$ predicts (measured over predicted at
$x = 1$: 0.69 to 0.88 for $D = 20$--$60$), and the maximum of $c$ at the centre approaches $1 - (\eps^3/2)\omega^2F'^2$,
their Eq.~(22) in this scaling (ratio 0.36 to 0.86). The same next-to-leading-order condition is used by Lai and
Wen~\cite{LaiWen2026}, who take it to select the period; the argument above applies to that use as well.

\section{Discussion}

The numerics give $\Delta(D)$ to high precision up to $D = 61$, and they favour a finite limit
$\Dinf = 1.64389375$ strongly, under the assumption of analyticity in $1/D$ that they support but cannot prove. The
theory explains why that number cannot come from the $1/D$ expansion: the expansion is unobstructed for every period,
and the selection acts through an exponentially small, Stokes-type effect whose linear version is solved exactly. The
inner problem at $x \sim \sqrt n$ is $n$-free; its higher-harmonic conditions already reproduce the shape of the echo
at $D = \infty$, and its harmonic-1 condition selects $\omega = (1.0000 \pm 0.0001)\,\omega_\infty$ untruncated and
$0.99923\,\omega_\infty$ truncated to five harmonics. Open are: higher orders of that condition, which would give $\Dinf$ (and possibly a closed
form) from first principles to more digits; a proof of Conjecture~\ref{conj} (a roadmap is in the repository's PROOF.md); the uniqueness of the $\lambda = 2$ mode and its $1/n$ correction;
and the behaviour just below $D = 4$. We also assume, without checking it, that the DSS branch we follow remains the
critical solution at large $D$, with a single unstable mode: our growth rate is a local root of the growth-mode
determinant near its predicted value, other unstable modes were not searched for, and no collapse has been evolved
above $D = 14$. If the branch acquired a second unstable mode, or a continuously self-similar solution~\cite{ClarkPimentel2025}
took over the threshold, $1/\lambda$ would not be the critical exponent.
