The problem, and how far it got

Tune the collapse of a massless scalar field to the threshold of black-hole formation, and the evolution approaches a universal solution that repeats itself on ever smaller scales. Each echo is a copy of the last, shrunk by eΔ. In four dimensions Δ ≈ 3.4454524. The project computes Δ as a function of the number D of spacetime dimensions.

It began in a chat on claude.ai. Nathan asked whether Claude could make discoveries in physics, and picked toy numerical relativity from the three projects it offered. The simulation, 177 full evolutions, reproduced Choptuik's mass scaling, but its Δ ≈ 3.30 fell short of 3.445 on a grid that resolved about one echo. A literature check then changed the target, since a 2026 paper had already computed Δ for continuous D between 3.05 and 5.5 (Ecker, Ecker, Grumiller and Jechtl). Computed directly as a periodic problem, the critical solution gave Δ = 3.4454523972 at D = 4, nine digits from the best published value, and the scan in D began.

Each point is a periodic boundary-value problem, solved by Newton's method from a prediction made at the last point. At large D the mass mode amplifies errors at the horizon by a gain G ≈ 0.7−(D−3), so the arithmetic had to carry more digits as D grew: double-double (about 32 digits), then quad-double (about 64), then octo-double (about 127). At low D one point costs 4 to 10 hours.

The large-D chain climbed for five weeks and was paused at D = 263.6, at about four hours per unit of D. Then a new formulation and direct solves at each target D reached 104 within a day.

The forty walls

A wall is a point where the work stalled or turned. For each, the idea that passed it is coded by who supplied it and by kind: physics or mathematics, a numerical method, computing (precision, speed, machines or a software defect), or process. Pick a wall to read it.

Walls 7 to 34: 28 in a row, none passed by a physical idea.

Who supplied the ideas, and what the prompts carried

Physics came only at the two ends.

In the chat's first two days Nathan suggested two mathematical forms, a sum and a repeating sum. The fits of the first held for a while, but its formulas did not last, and the second was tested and rejected. The coordinator's one physical idea came on the first day: the condition that selects Δ weakens exponentially in D. The next 28 walls, over five weeks, were all passed by numerical methods, computing or process.

The last three physical ideas came from subagents in the final two days: the chain's limits as the discrete system's own, the momentum constraint, and the anti-Stokes point. Each followed a prompt of his asking for an outside look. With them, direct solves went from a stalled chain to D = 104 within a day.

The coordinator passed most walls, 22 of the 40, 14 of them with a numerical method. Nine of his eleven goals were answered by its ideas, and so were all three of his choices among its options. All six of his requests for an outside look were answered by a subagent. His own ideas were about tools and the work: SIMD and Rust, a new desktop, the jump test, the raw-data rule and the pause, beside the two forms that did not last.

The prompts

Most prompts were short. A prompt that changed the course looked like any other: about as short and as often an instruction. Questions that propose, such as Could it be a sum?, were more common among them, and go-aheads and check-ins almost never turned the work.

Three walls up close

One from the first day, one from the middle, and the last run of walls, which took the work from a stalled chain to D = 104.

The stall near D = 9.3

walls 2 to 3 · 18 October · the chat
  1. 2day 0

    Nathan set the target, a formula, and kept pushing: Yes continue to find the formula you are doing great don't give up. It exists, then Could it be a sum? Claude turned each suggested form into a fit tested on dimensions it had not seen. A fit to D ≤ 8 predicted the computed values out to D = 10.1 within 2×10⁻⁵. That habit later became the held-out and planted-truth tests.

  2. 3day 0

    The scan stalled near D = 9.3. Claude said it would look into it later, and he wrote Continue searching.

  3. result

    The diagnosis turned the stall into a finding. The condition that selects Δ weakens exponentially with D, so no plain series in 1/D can fix Δ, and double precision could fix Δ only to about 10⁻⁴ near D = 10. The idea became Proposition 1 of the later work.

  4. later

    The fitted formulas did not last. The first one for Δ fell like (D−2)−1/4, while the later data give a finite limit, Δ∞ = 1.643893746.

The wall at 78

walls 17 to 20 · 27 October to 3 November
  1. 17day 9

    The horizon block stopped the chain past 73. After continue to D = 200 and try to skip past 73 by a larger number., new node layouts reached 78.1, and Newton's basin was measured. The walls began to read as limits of the formulation, not of the solution.

  2. 19day 15

    For a week the frontier sat at 78.1. He wrote just jump to 100. Jumps of every size, from two starts, followed. None landed past 79.3, which put the wall in Newton's basin rather than in the step rule. One cheap experiment settled it.

  3. 20day 16

    Collocation and a hybrid failed. After I'm still not certain why we can't get past 78 D, a literature subagent brought back Taylor continuation in D, the asymptotic numerical method (Cochelin 1994). It took the chain from 73.27 to 79.27.

  4. changed

    Search the literature before building. The next wall, the double-double wall near 80, fell to a quad-double corrector and jets.

The mass mode read as physics

walls 31 to 37 · 6 to 25 November
  1. 31day 19

    Quad-double hit its floor at 193.77. A literature note had called the solver's mass mode physical, a property of Einstein's equations, and for three weeks it was met with more digits: a stall rule with a flag, then an octo-double corrector.

  2. 32–34days 20–34

    Keep going to 400 sent the chain to 263.6. A direction review he asked for counted 185 of the 450 issues closed in the preceding week as the chain, and recommended stopping it. Two days later he paused it: a unit of D cost four hours, and D = 400 was 23 days away.

  3. 35day 37

    Can you re-evaluate the underlying math assumptions to see if we missed anything? A subagent derived the large-D problem before reading the project's notes, and found the chain's limits were the discrete system's own.

  4. 36day 38

    I think you may have already lost the plot. … At these speeds it is unobtainable. The coordinator answered that the mistake was treating large D as a continuation problem, and set up direct solves at each target D.

  5. 37day 38

    He asked for subagents to look for gaps in the published papers. One found that the mass mode is a violation of the momentum constraint, which the formulation never imposed. Gundlach's construction sets the metric from that constraint and has no such mode. A prototype in that form converged at D = 100 in double-double arithmetic.

  6. result

    One solve at D = 300 converged four hours after his call for a new method, and 250 and 400 followed within twenty minutes. Walls 38 to 40 took it on to 1000, 2000, 2828 and 104, all within 24 hours of that call.

In hindsight, the one wrong step was a label.

With D = 1000 in hand he asked whether a single prompt could have got there sooner. The analysis answered partly. Gundlach's paper was already cited in the repository, and a check of the momentum constraint existed in another part of the code. A prompt asking which equation allows the mode, and how the published constructions set each metric field, could have reached the new form about 18 days sooner, by its estimate. The Möbius variable at the horizon could not have come from that prompt, since its symptom stayed hidden until a solve at D = 1000 was tried.

One prompt, one session

The setup above is costly and cannot be rerun as it was. Can one prompt to one fresh session reproduce the start? A clean-room trial tested it. One headless Claude Code session, with the model Fable 5.2, ran in a sandbox with an empty folder and Python with numpy, scipy, mpmath and sympy. It had no project files, no memory, no instruction file and no web tools. Canary runs checked what a session can see, and one planted an instruction file, which the session had to report. It did.

The prompt has 146 words. It names no paper, method, equation or value, and it forbids looking values up or quoting them from memory.

nathan › Compute the echoing period Δ of the Choptuik critical solution in D = 4, 6, 7 and 8 spacetime dimensions, each correct to six decimal places. The critical solution is the discretely self-similar solution of general relativity with a spherically symmetric, minimally coupled, massless real scalar field that sits at the threshold of black hole formation. Δ is its period in logarithmic time. Work from first principles in the current directory: derive the equations, write the solver, and test your own results for convergence. Do not look the values up, and do not quote them from memory. Python 3 with numpy, scipy, mpmath and sympy is installed. You have 4 CPU threads. When you are done, write two files: - results.csv, with the header D,Delta and one row for each D; - METHOD.md, which says how each value was computed and what its convergence test showed.

Its notes give its route: the field equations in Bondi coordinates, checked symbolically; a time evolution at D = 4 with a bisection of the amplitude for a first guess; a spectral Newton solve with Δ as an unknown; continuation in the dimension; and convergence tables.

The same prompt, widened to every whole D from 6 to 200 with 12 hours allowed, ran for 2 h 11 min before Nathan stopped it for its token use. It had six values by then, for D = 6 to 11, from a coarse grid. Five were right to six decimals, and D = 9 was off by 1.9×10⁻⁶.

The opening of the project, from the statement of the problem to nine digits at D = 4, fits in one prompt anyone can rerun. The walls lie past what the trials reached. How far one session reaches in D is not measured.

Lessons

  1. Push on the goal, and let the method be argued.
  2. Ask why at every wall, and turn the answer into a measurement.
  3. Prefer a cheap decisive experiment to a long plan.
  4. Build the next tool before it is needed.
  5. Write the stop rule and the pass criteria before the run, and keep the control runnable.
  6. Report the time to each goal at the current rate. When it passes about a week, change the method rather than repair it.
  7. When a method has stalled for days, ask for a review that derives the problem from the start before it reads the project's notes.
  8. Before building around a cost that grows, have the agent show which equation allows it.
  9. Ask the literature how it sets the problem up, not only how to pass the wall.

What this does not show

One coder

The forty walls were coded once, by the Claude session that drafted the paper's section, from the appendix rows and their sources. No second coder has repeated it. The kinds overlap, and another reader would draw the lines elsewhere.

Incomplete records

The chat's 523 prompts kept no times, and the kinds of push read only its first 62. The runners' transcripts stayed on their machines. The per-point records of 757 early points were lost.

One project, one person

The counts say which pushes were common, not which mattered most. The form rules misread about 3 prompts in 100.

One run of each trial

The model is not deterministic, so the rate of success is unknown. A model may also have seen published values in training. The session's working is evidence that it computed its values, not proof.

No expert review

No expert has reviewed the work, and Nathan has not checked the mathematics himself. The checks, each with a control that must fail, make the work testable. They do not replace a reviewer.

How this was made

Nathan Moinvaziri is the author of the work and of this page. Claude, Anthropic's model, used through Claude Code, was the tool. It produced the code, the computations and the notes, as a coordinating session, runner sessions and subagents under his direction. Claude drafted the paper behind this page and this page itself from the chat where the project began, the repository, its issues and the session transcripts, and he read and revised each draft.

In the first week Nathan wrote to the authors of the closest published work and asked that the email state plainly how the work was done.
