\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Choptuik's critical solution~\cite{Choptuik1993} is the spacetime at the threshold of black-hole formation in the
spherically symmetric collapse of a massless scalar field. It is discretely self-similar (DSS): in suitable
coordinates it repeats itself on scales smaller by a fixed factor $e^{\Delta}$, and $\Delta$ is called the echo period.
As a mathematical object it is the solution of a nonlinear eigenvalue problem~\cite{Gundlach1995}: a solution of the
self-similar Einstein--scalar equations that is periodic in logarithmic time, regular at the centre and analytic at the
past light cone of the singularity exists only for isolated values of $\Delta$. In four spacetime dimensions
$\Delta = 3.445452402(3)$~\cite{MartinGarciaGundlach2003}, and Reiterer and Trubowitz proved the existence of the solution
by a computer-assisted argument that encloses $\Delta$ to about $80$ digits~\cite{ReitererTrubowitz2019}. The problem
can be posed in any number $D = n + 2$ of spacetime dimensions, with $n$ entering the equations as a parameter; see the
review~\cite{GundlachReview2025} for the physics.

This paper is about the limit $n \to \infty$. In the companion paper~\cite{companion} the critical solution is
computed up to $D = 61$, and the echo period approaches a finite limit $\Dinf = 1.64389375$ (Section~\ref{sec:numbers}).
The questions here are mathematical. Why does the expansion of the equations in powers of $1/n$ not determine $\Dinf$?
What does? And what can be proved about it?

The answer has the structure of a classical selection problem of exponential asymptotics. In Saffman--Taylor finger
selection and in dendritic growth, a formal expansion in a small parameter exists for every value of the quantity to
be selected, and an actual solution exists only when an exponentially small term vanishes; that term is the Stokes
multiplier of an inner equation posed near a complex singular point~\cite{Combescot1986,HongLanger1986,KruskalSegur1991}.
Xie and Tanveer made this rigorous~\cite{XieTanveer2003,TanveerXie2003,Xie2019}. In our problem the small parameter is
$1/n$, the selected quantities are the frequency $\omega = 2\pi/\Delta$ and the time profile of the solution, the
symmetry condition is analyticity at the light cone, and the complex singular point is the point at infinity of the
self-similar radius. The new feature is that the unknown depends on two variables, the radius and a periodic phase, so
that the inner problem is an infinite system coupled through harmonics; neither of the templates we follow has that.

\subsection*{Results}
We work within a scaling ansatz for large $n$ that is measured on the numerical solutions (Ansatz~\ref{ans:scaling}).
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item \emph{The $1/n$ expansion is unobstructed} (Proposition~\ref{prop:1}). The formal expansion exists to all orders for
every period and every time profile, so the period is selected beyond all orders in $1/n$.
\item \emph{The leading order is a free wave solved by ${}_2F_1$} (Proposition~\ref{prop:2}). Its solution regular at the
centre has a non-analytic branch at the light cone with an explicit coefficient $C(n,s)$, a ratio of Gamma functions that
never vanishes.
\item \emph{Gravity transports the fast WKB mode by a non-vanishing factor} (Proposition~\ref{prop:3}), so inside the
light cone gravity cannot cancel the non-analytic piece.
\item \emph{The late orders of the leading-order $1/n$ series encode $C(n,s)$ as a Stokes multiplier}
(Proposition~\ref{prop:4}), of an exponential that is born at the outer singular point $x = \infty$ and switched on along
the real axis beyond the light cone.
\item \emph{An $n$-free inner problem} lives at $x \sim \sqrt n$. Up to a change of variables it is the leading-order
large-$D$ system of Rozali and Way~\cite{RozaliWay2018}. Without gravity its Stokes constant is Weber's, and it reproduces
$C(n,s)$ for large $n$. We state the selection rule as Conjecture~\ref{conj}: the critical solution at $D = \infty$ is
selected by the vanishing of the fast-type Stokes multipliers of the inner problem. We give the argument for it, its
hypotheses, and the three places where rigour fails.
\item \emph{An exact unstable mode} (Proposition~\ref{prop:5}). Written in $s = r^2/(2n)$ the inner problem is an
autonomous nonlinear heat equation, and the shift of $s$, gauge-fixed, is an exact perturbation with growth rate
$\lambda = 2$ whose Stokes multipliers vanish with those of the background. Under Conjecture~\ref{conj}, uniqueness of
the unstable mode and continuity of $\lambda$ in $1/n$, the critical exponent tends to $1/2$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:gamma}).
\item \emph{A selection theorem for $M$-harmonic models} (Theorem~\ref{thm:B}), following Xie and Tanveer, reduced to two
contraction arguments; the other lemmas are proved or cited.
\item \emph{A computer-assisted proof} (Theorem~\ref{thm:A}): for the one-harmonic truncation of the inner problem, the
selection condition has exactly one zero in an explicit box of half-width $10^{-9}$, and it is simple. The proof
assumes Braaksma's summability theorem and nothing else from the literature. It follows the template of Baldom\'a,
Capi\'nski, Guardia and Seara~\cite{BaldomaCapinskiGuardiaSeara2023}: a start-value fixed point, a validated Lohner
integration, a Krawczyk test, a lemma that one component of the jump at one point is the whole jump, and a covering
of an annular sector by 178 validated Taylor discs.
\item \emph{The untruncated multiplier} behaves numerically as an entire function of the squared amplitude, of
exponential type (Section~\ref{sec:entire}); its first coefficient is enclosed rigorously.
\end{enumerate}
Evaluated numerically, the selection rule gives $\omega/\winf$ between $0.99915$ and $1.00011$ from twelve orders of an
amplitude expansion, and $\Dinf = 1.6439 \pm 0.0002$ from the $D = \infty$ equations alone. This is evidence for
Conjecture~\ref{conj}, not a proof, and the $\pm$ is the range of several estimators, not an error bound
(Section~\ref{sec:evidence}). Table~\ref{tab:status} lists the statements and their status.

\subsection*{Prior work}
The leading-order freedom of the period, any periodic profile with any period, was found by Ecker, Ecker and
Grumiller~\cite{EckerEckerGrumiller2026}, who constructed analytic DSS solutions at large $D$ in the same gauge;
Proposition~\ref{prop:1} extends it to all orders. The free wave equation in similarity coordinates was analysed in $3+1$
dimensions by Donninger~\cite{Donninger2009}, which contains the $D = 4$ case of Proposition~\ref{prop:2}; the
coefficient $C(n,s)$ follows from Gauss's connection formula~\cite[Eq.~15.8.4]{DLMF} once the mode equation is recognised
as hypergeometric, and we did not find it for general $D$ in the literature we searched. Proposition~\ref{prop:4} is an
explicit instance of Dingle's late-order analysis~\cite{Dingle1973}, whose nonlinear form is due to Chapman, King and
Adams~\cite{ChapmanKingAdams1998}; the analogous singularity structure for ${}_2F_1$ with large parameters is worked out
by Paris~\cite{Paris2013}. Trans-series corrections in $1/n$ are familiar in large-$D$ gravity~\cite{EmparanHerzog2020}.
Rozali and Way~\cite{RozaliWay2018} derived the $D$-free leading-order equations that are our inner problem, noted that
they are parabolic with the roles of space and time swapped, and estimated $\gamma = 1/2$ from the divergence of their
oscillating solutions. What is new here is their use for the discretely self-similar solution: the inner Stokes
multipliers, the selection conjecture and the symmetry argument of Proposition~\ref{prop:5}. Clark and
Pimentel~\cite{ClarkPimentel2025} constructed continuously self-similar critical solutions at large $D$ by matched
asymptotics. The templates for the proofs are Xie and Tanveer for the selection theorem, Baldom\'a, Fontich, Guardia and
Seara~\cite{BaldomaFontichGuardiaSeara} for rigorous inner--outer matching and continuation, and Baldom\'a, Capi\'nski,
Guardia and Seara~\cite{BaldomaCapinskiGuardiaSeara2023} for the computer-assisted Stokes constant. Other rigorous
instances of ``existence if and only if an exponentially small coefficient vanishes'' are in Lombardi's
monograph~\cite{Lombardi2000}.

\section{Setting}
\label{sec:setting}

\subsection{The self-similar equations}
Let $D = n + 2$ be the spacetime dimension and $\eps = 1/n$. We use the self-similar radius $x$ (areal radius over time
to the singularity, normalized so that the past light cone of the singularity is at $x = 1$), logarithmic time
$\tau = -\ln(-t)$, and the phase $\theta = \omega\tau$, with $\omega = 2\pi/\Delta$. The unknowns are four functions of
$(x, \theta)$, $2\pi$-periodic in $\theta$: $p$ and $m$, the scaled derivatives of the scalar field along the two radial
null directions; $b = a^2$, with $a$ the radial metric function; and $c$, the lapse-type function that sets the light
cones (the past light cone is where $x = c$). With $G = (n-1)\,c\,(b-1)$, the self-similar Einstein--scalar equations
read~\cite{companion}
\begin{align}
x c_x &= (n-1)\,c\,(b-1), \qquad\qquad x b_x = b\,(p^2+m^2) - (n-1)\,b\,(b-1), \label{eq:E12}\\
x(x-c)\,p_x &= -\omega x\,p_\theta + G p - c p + \tfrac{n}{2}\,c\,(p+m), \label{eq:E3}\\
x(x+c)\,m_x &= -\omega x\,m_\theta - G m + c m - \tfrac{n}{2}\,c\,(p+m). \label{eq:E4}
\end{align}

\begin{definition}[the DSS problem]\label{def:dss}
Find $\omega > 0$ and functions $p, m, b, c$ on $0 \le x \le 1$, $2\pi$-periodic in $\theta$, with only odd harmonics of
$\theta$ in $p, m$ and only even ones in $b, c$, that solve \eqref{eq:E12}--\eqref{eq:E4} and are
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item regular at the centre $x = 0$,
\item analytic at the past light cone $x = 1$, and
\item normalized by $c(1) = 1$ (the gauge that puts the light cone at $x = 1$).
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Both $x = 0$ and $x = c = 1$ are regular singular points of \eqref{eq:E3}--\eqref{eq:E4}. Regularity at the centre
removes one branch at $x = 0$; at $x = 1$ the general solution has a branch $(1-x)^{\Lambda}$ with non-integer $\Lambda$,
and requiring its coefficients to vanish is what selects $\omega$, as in~\cite{Gundlach1995}. The centre series has the
exact symmetry $m(x) = p(-x)$.

\subsection{Numerical input}
\label{sec:numbers}
The companion paper~\cite{companion} computes the critical solution at 857 values $8.25 \le D \le 61.03$ in
double-double arithmetic. If $\Delta$ is analytic in $1/D$, which held-out tests there support but do not prove,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Dinf}
\Dinf = 1.64389375 \pm 1.5\times10^{-8}, \qquad \winf = 2\pi/\Dinf = 3.82213589 \pm 4\times10^{-8},
\end{equation}
where the $\pm$ is the spread over 48 fit choices. The growth rate $\lambda$ of the unstable mode, $\lambda = 1/\gamma$
with $\gamma$ the critical exponent of the black-hole radius, $r_{\rm BH} \propto (p - p^*)^{\gamma}$~\cite{KoikeHaraAdachi1995},
tends to $2$: free fits in $1/n$ put $\lambda_\infty = 2$ to about $10^{-6}$, so $\gamma \to 1/2$ to about $5\times10^{-7}$,
and with $\lambda_\infty = 2$ imposed $\lambda = 2 + 3.0324/n + \dots$. The time profile at the centre,
$F_n(\theta) = \sqrt n\,\partial_x p(0, \theta)$, converges as $D$ grows: its first harmonic falls from $3.480$ at $D = 20$
to $2.962$ at $D = 60$ and extrapolates to about $2.72$, and $n\,(1 - \langle c(0)\rangle)$ and $\langle F^2\rangle$
approach a common limit near $3.7$ (angle brackets denote the mean over $\theta$). None of these numbers is used in a
proof below. Where an exact value of $\winf$ is needed, as in Theorem~\ref{thm:A}, we \emph{define}
$\winf := 2\pi/1.64389375$; this is a convention, and the theorem is equally a statement about $\omega$ itself.

\subsection{The scaling at large \texorpdfstring{$n$}{n}}
\label{sec:ansatz}
On the double-double solutions at $D = 12$--$60$~\cite{companion}: $p \approx x F(\theta)/\sqrt n$ near the centre, and
$p + m$ is smaller than $p$ by a further factor of order $1/n$; $c = 1 - O(1/n)$; and $b - 1 = (p^2+m^2)/(n-1)$ to
about $3\%$. Write $p = \eps^{1/2}P$, $m = \eps^{1/2}M$, $S = P + M$ and $\Delta_P = P - M$. The sum and the difference of
\eqref{eq:E3} and \eqref{eq:E4} are
\begin{align}
x\,c\,\partial_x \Delta_P - c\,\Delta_P &= x^2 \partial_x S + \omega x\,\partial_\theta S - \tfrac{1-\eps}{\eps}\, c\,(b-1)\,\Delta_P ,
\label{eq:sum}\\
S &= \eps\,\frac{x^2 \partial_x \Delta_P - x c\,\partial_x S + \omega x\,\partial_\theta \Delta_P}{c\,(1-\eps)\,b} .
\label{eq:diff}
\end{align}

\begin{ansatz}[scaling]\label{ans:scaling}
The fields have formal expansions in integer powers of $\eps$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ansatz}
S = \sum_{k\ge1}\eps^k S_k,\quad \Delta_P = \sum_{k\ge0}\eps^k D_k,\quad c = 1 - \sum_{k\ge1}\eps^k G_k,\quad
b = 1 + \sum_{k\ge2}\eps^k B_k,\quad \omega = \sum_{k\ge0}\eps^k\omega_k ,
\end{equation}
with coefficients that are functions of $(x, \theta)$, $2\pi$-periodic in $\theta$.
\end{ansatz}

The scaling is forced by one measured fact, $c - 1 = O(1/n)$ (gravity is weak; $n(1 - \langle c(0)\rangle) \to 3.65$).
The $b$ equation gives $b - 1 \approx (p^2+m^2)/(n-1)$; the $c$ equation then gives $c - 1 = O(n(b-1)) = O(p^2)$, so
$c - 1 = O(1/n)$ requires $p = O(n^{-1/2})$; and the terms of order $n$ in \eqref{eq:E3}--\eqref{eq:E4} force
$p + m = O(p/n)$, which is the measured $p + m = O(n^{-3/2})$. Integer powers of $\eps$ beyond the overall $\eps^{1/2}$
are an assumption (analyticity in $1/n$), consistent with the held-out tests on $\Delta(D)$ in~\cite{companion} but not
implied by them. All statements of Section~\ref{sec:outer} about the formal expansion hold within this ansatz; whether the
formal family is the asymptotic expansion of actual solutions is hypothesis (H2) of Conjecture~\ref{conj}.

\section{The outer expansion and its late orders}
\label{sec:outer}

\subsection{The \texorpdfstring{$1/n$}{1/n} expansion is unobstructed}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:1}
Let $F(\theta)$ be any smooth $2\pi$-periodic function with odd harmonics only, $\omega_0 \neq 0$, $\omega_1, \omega_2,
\dots$ any numbers, and $K_1(\theta), K_2(\theta), \dots$ any smooth odd-harmonic functions. Then
\eqref{eq:sum}--\eqref{eq:diff} together with \eqref{eq:E12} have exactly one formal solution of the form
\eqref{eq:ansatz} with $D_0 = 2xF$ in which, at every order,
\begin{itemize}
\item $S_k$ is an even polynomial in $x$ starting at $x^2$,
\item $D_k - xK_k$ is an odd polynomial starting at $x^3$, and
\item $G_k$ and $B_{k+1}$ are even polynomials ($B$ starting at $x^2$) with $G_k(1) = 0$,
\end{itemize}
all with smooth periodic coefficients. Every order is regular at the centre and analytic at the light cone, and
$m(x) = p(-x)$ holds order by order. (The hypothesis $\omega_0 \ne 0$ only excludes the static case; the proof does not
use it.)
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By induction on $k$, with the structure in the statement as the hypothesis for all orders below $k$. Write $E$ for the
even polynomials in $x$ with smooth periodic coefficients.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),leftmargin=2em]
\item \emph{$B_{k+1}$.} The $b$ equation at order $k+1$ gives $B_{k+1}$ algebraically, since its coefficient is
$(n-1)b \approx 1/\eps$, from $x\partial_x B_k$, $(1+B)(P^2+M^2)$ and $(1-\eps)(1+B)B$ at lower orders. Since
$P^2 + M^2 = (S^2 + \Delta_P^2)/2$ with $S \in x^2E$ and $\Delta_P \in xE$, every term lies in $x^2E$.
\item \emph{$G_k$.} The $c$ equation at order $k+1$ reads $-x\,\partial_x G_k = [(1-\eps)\,c\,(b-1)]_{k+1} \in x^2E$.
Dividing by $x$ leaves only odd powers $x^{2j-1}$, $j \ge 1$, so $G_k = -\sum_j r_{2j}x^{2j}/(2j) + \text{const}$ is an
even polynomial without $\log x$. The constant is fixed by $G_k(1) = 0$.
\item \emph{$S_k$.} The numerator $x^2\partial_x\Delta_P - xc\,\partial_x S + \omega x\,\partial_\theta\Delta_P$ of
\eqref{eq:diff} maps $\Delta_P \in xE$, $S \in x^2E$ into $x^2E$. The denominator is $1 + O(\eps)$ with coefficients in
$E$, so its inverse is a power series in $\eps$ with coefficients in $E$. Hence $S_k \in x^2E$.
\item \emph{$D_k$.} Equation \eqref{eq:sum} at order $k$ is $x\,\partial_x D_k - D_k = R_k$. Here $R_k$ collects
$x^2\partial_x S_k + \omega x\,\partial_\theta S_k \in x^3E$, terms $c\,(b-1)\,\Delta_P \in x^3E$, and terms
$G_i\,(x\,\partial_x D_j - D_j)$; the last are in $x^3E$ because $x\partial_x - 1$ annihilates $xK_j$. So $R_k$ is odd and
starts at $x^3$, and
\[
D_k = x\int R_k\,x^{-2}\,\dd x = \sum_{j \ge 1} \frac{R_{k,2j+1}}{2j}\, x^{2j+1} + xK_k .
\]
The only possible obstruction, a term $x^1$ in $R_k$ that would produce $x \log x$ (not allowed at the centre), never
occurs.
\item \emph{Symmetry.} $S$ even and $\Delta_P$ odd in $x$ is the same as $p(-x) = m(x)$.
\item \emph{Harmonic content.} If $P, M$ (hence $S, \Delta_P$) contain only odd harmonics of $\theta$ and $B, G$ only even
ones at the lower orders, every step above preserves this: products of two odd-harmonic factors are even-harmonic,
products of an odd and an even one are odd-harmonic, and $\partial_\theta$ keeps the harmonic.
\end{enumerate}
Polynomials are analytic everywhere, so the light-cone condition holds automatically, and $c(1) = 1$ holds by
construction. Nothing in the construction constrains $F$, $\omega_0$ or the $\omega_k$.
\end{proof}

The leading-order part of this freedom, any periodic profile with any period, is in~\cite{EckerEckerGrumiller2026}.
The first orders (computed symbolically to fourth order, \repo{solver/largeD\_expand.py}) are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:firstorders}
P_0 = xF,\quad B_2 = 2x^2F^2,\quad G_1 = F^2\,(1-x^2),\quad S_1 = 2x^2\,(F + \omega_0 F'),\quad
D_1 = x^3\,(\omega_0^2 F'' + 3\omega_0 F' + 2F - 2F^3) + xK_1 .
\end{equation}
Substituting the expansion through $S_3$, $G_3$, $B_4$ into the exact equations, with $F$ from the $D = 60$ solution, the
relative residual of every equation falls like $n^{-3}$ for $n = 50, \dots, 800$, both for $\omega_0 = 2\pi/1.64389$ and
for $\omega_0 = 2\pi/1.4$ (\repo{solver/largeD\_formal\_check.py}).

\begin{remark}[selection beyond all orders]\label{rem:beyond}
If $\Delta(D)$ is analytic in $1/D$, its expansion coefficients cannot be computed order by order from the field
equations: within Ansatz~\ref{ans:scaling} the selection acts through terms smaller than every power of $1/n$. The same
holds for the critical exponent. The expansion of growing perturbations $\propto e^{\lambda\tau}$ of the formal solution
goes through for every $\lambda$, since the induction above works with every $\tau$-derivative of a perturbation replaced
by $\lambda + \omega\partial_\theta$ (checked symbolically through order 2, \repo{solver/inner\_pert/expand\_pert.py}). So
$\lambda = 1/\gamma$ is also selected beyond all orders.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The leading-order problem}
At leading order ($c = b = 1$) the equations are the scalar wave equation on flat space in self-similar form. For one
harmonic $e^{\iu\mu\theta}$ ($\mu$ odd) put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:abk}
s = \iu\mu\omega,\qquad \alpha = \tfrac{1+s}{2},\qquad \beta = \tfrac{2+s}{2},\qquad \kappa = \tfrac{n+1}{2}.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:2}
The solution of the leading-order problem that is regular at the centre is, up to a constant,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prop2sol}
p - m = x\,{}_2F_1(\alpha,\beta;\kappa;x^2), \qquad
p + m = \frac{x(1-x^2)\,\partial_x(p-m) - (1+sx^2)\,(p-m)}{x\,(s-n+1)} .
\end{equation}
Near the light cone it contains the non-analytic branch $(1-x^2)^{n/2-1-s}$ with coefficient
\begin{equation}\label{eq:C}
C(n,s) = \frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\!\left(s+1-\frac n2\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1+s}{2}\right)\Gamma\!\left(\frac{2+s}{2}\right)},
\end{equation}
which is finite and non-zero for every real $\omega \ne 0$, odd $\mu$ and $n \ge 2$. Hence no time-periodic self-similar
solution of the linear problem is analytic at the light cone, in any dimension $D \ge 4$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
With $c = b = 1$ the difference equation gives $(n - 1)\,(p+m) = x^2\,\partial_x(p-m) - x\,\partial_x(p+m) + sx\,(p-m)$,
and the sum gives $x\,\partial_x(p-m) - (p-m) = x^2\,\partial_x(p+m) + sx\,(p+m)$. Eliminating $p + m$ gives, for
$y = p - m$,
\[
x^2(1-x^2)\,y'' + x\,\big(n - 2 - 2(s+1)x^2\big)\,y' - \big(n - 2 + s(s+1)x^2\big)\,y = 0 ,
\]
and with $y = x\,w(z)$, $z = x^2$, this is Gauss's equation $z(1-z)w'' + [\kappa - (\alpha+\beta+1)z]\,w' - \alpha\beta\,w = 0$.
Its exponents are $0$ and $1-\kappa$ at $z = 0$, so $y \sim x$ or $y \sim x^{2-n}$; for $n > 2$ regularity, and for $n = 2$
parity ($y = p - m$ is odd in $x$ because $m(x) = p(-x)$, while $x^0$ is even), selects ${}_2F_1(\alpha,\beta;\kappa;z)$.
At $z = 1$ the exponents are $0$ and $\kappa - \alpha - \beta = n/2 - 1 - s$. Since $s$ is imaginary and non-zero,
$\kappa - \alpha - \beta$ is not an integer, and Gauss's connection formula~\cite[Eq.~15.8.4]{DLMF}
\[
{}_2F_1(\alpha,\beta;\kappa;z) = \frac{\Gamma(\kappa)\Gamma(\kappa-\alpha-\beta)}{\Gamma(\kappa-\alpha)\Gamma(\kappa-\beta)}\,{}_2F_1(\alpha,\beta;\alpha+\beta-\kappa+1;1-z)
+ (1-z)^{\kappa-\alpha-\beta}\,\frac{\Gamma(\kappa)\Gamma(\alpha+\beta-\kappa)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\,{}_2F_1(\kappa-\alpha,\kappa-\beta;\kappa-\alpha-\beta+1;1-z)
\]
applies. The second coefficient is $C(n,s)$. The Gamma function has no zeros, and $\alpha$, $\beta$ and
$\alpha + \beta - \kappa$ are not poles, because their imaginary parts are $\mu\omega/2$, $\mu\omega/2$ and $\mu\omega$,
all non-zero.
\end{proof}

At $40$ digits the formula satisfies the original first-order equations to $10^{-40}$ for $n = 18$--$1000$. For the
fundamental at $\omega = \winf$, $|C| = 0.0121$, $0.0114$, $0.0124$, $0.0192$, $0.0405$ at $n = 18$, $38$, $58$, $200$,
$1000$: it grows like $\sqrt n$ and is small only through a factor of order $e^{-\pi\mu\omega/2}$. Without gravity a
free wave cannot be both regular at the centre and analytic at the light cone. The branch $(1-x^2)^{n/2-1-s}$ is smaller
than any power of $1/n$ at every fixed $x < 1$, which is why Proposition~\ref{prop:1} never sees it; gravity, through
the $O(1/n)$ couplings, has to cancel its coefficient harmonic by harmonic. The next two subsections show that it cannot
do so inside $0 < x < 1$, and locate where the cancellation must happen instead.

\subsection{What gravity does to the non-analytic piece}
The non-analytic piece is a fast WKB mode: in the exact solution of Proposition~\ref{prop:2} it is
$x^{2-n}(1-x^2)^{n/2-1-s}$, which varies on the scale $1/n$. Put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Phi}
\Phi(x) = \ln x - \tfrac12\ln(1-x^2) .
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:3}
Let $F(\theta)$ and $\omega$ be given, and $c = 1 - \eps F^2(1-x^2) + O(\eps^2)$ as in Proposition~\ref{prop:1}. The
perturbations
\[
\delta p = e^{-n\Phi(x)}\big(A(x,\theta) + O(1/n)\big), \qquad
\delta m = e^{-n\Phi(x)}\Big(\frac{1-x}{1+x}\,A(x,\theta) + O(1/n)\Big)
\]
solve the equations linearized about the formal solution on $0 < x < 1$, to the order shown, if and only if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:transport}
x(1-x^2)\,\partial_x A - 2\omega x^2\,\partial_\theta A = \big(1 + x - 2x^2F(\theta)^2\big)\,A ,
\end{equation}
that is, along the characteristics $\theta = \theta_0 + \omega\ln(1-x^2)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:transportsol}
A = A_0(\theta_0)\,\frac{x}{1-x}\,\exp\!\Big(-\int_0^x \frac{2t\,F\big(\theta_0 + \omega\ln(1-t^2)\big)^2}{1-t^2}\,\dd t\Big) .
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Substitute into the linearized equations. The terms of order $n$ come from $x(x\mp c)\,\partial_x$ acting on
$e^{-n\Phi}$ and from $\tfrac n2 c\,(\delta p+\delta m)$. With $B$ the amplitude of $\delta m$ they read
\[
-x(x-1)\,\Phi'\,A = \tfrac12 (A+B), \qquad -x(x+1)\,\Phi'\,B = -\tfrac12 (A+B) .
\]
A non-zero solution needs $x(1-x^2)\,\Phi' = 1$, which gives $\Phi$, and then $B = A(1-x)/(1+x)$. At order $1$ the
corrections $(a_1, b_1)$ satisfy a linear system with the same singular matrix,
\[
\frac12\begin{pmatrix} \frac{1-x}{1+x} & -1 \\[2pt] 1 & -\frac{1+x}{1-x} \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ b_1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} R_p \\ R_m \end{pmatrix},
\]
whose left null vector is $(1+x, -(1-x))$. The right-hand sides collect $x(x\mp1)\,\partial_x$ of $A$ and $B$, the
$\omega x\,\partial_\theta$ terms, the $\mp c$ terms, and the gravity terms from $c = 1 - \eps G_1$ inside
$x(x\mp c)\,n\Phi'$ and $\tfrac n2 c$. The perturbations of $b$ and $c$, and $G\,\delta p$, are smaller by $1/n$ and drop
out. Solvability, $(1+x)R_p - (1-x)R_m = 0$, multiplied by $(1+x)/2$, is \eqref{eq:transport}; with
$G_1 = F^2(1-x^2)$ the gravity terms add up to $-2x^2F^2A$. A first-order equation integrates along its characteristics,
and $\int (1+x)/(x(1-x^2))\,\dd x = \ln\big(x/(1-x)\big)$ gives the prefactor.
\end{proof}

Without gravity a harmonic $A = \alpha(x)e^{\iu\mu\theta}$ gives $\alpha = x(1-x)^{-1}(1-x^2)^{-s}$ and
$\delta p - \delta m = 2x^{2-n}(1-x^2)^{n/2-1-s}$: exactly the second solution of Proposition~\ref{prop:2}, with its
exponents at the centre and at the light cone. A symbolic repetition of the derivation from the exact equations, with
$\delta b$ and $\delta c$ kept (\repo{solver/largeD\_transport\_sympy.py}), confirms that they enter only at order
$\eps^{3/2}e^{-n\Phi}$. Integrating the linearized equations directly from $x = 0.9$ to $0.72$
($F = 2.72\sin\theta + 0.22\sin3\theta$, $\omega = \winf$) reproduces the transported $A$ with relative errors $0.57$,
$0.26$, $0.13$, $0.062$, $0.031$ at $n = 100$, $200$, $400$, $800$, $1600$, i.e.\ like $1/n$. Three consequences:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2em]
\item Gravity acts on the non-analytic piece at order $1$, not $1/n$ (over the interval above it multiplies it by about
$40$). Near the light cone the characteristic winds through all phases and the factor behaves like
$(1-x^2)^{\langle F^2\rangle}$, so the light-cone exponent becomes $n/2 - 1 - s + \langle F^2\rangle$. With
$\langle F^2\rangle \approx 3.7$, an expansion in the amplitude of the wave is not a route to the selection.
\item The factor in \eqref{eq:transportsol} never vanishes, so gravity cannot cancel the fast mode on the way to the
light cone.
\item Every characteristic reaches $x = 0$, where the fast mode is the singular branch $x^{2-n}$. A solution regular at
the centre therefore has $A_0 = 0$ on every characteristic: at any order of this WKB expansion it carries no fast mode on
$0 < x < 1$. The non-analytic piece must come from a Stokes phenomenon.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The light-cone coefficient as a Stokes multiplier}
A divergent series records the exponentially small terms it switches on in its late
orders~\cite{Dingle1973,ChapmanKingAdams1998}: if the switched-on term is $n^{-g}e^{-n\chi(x)}$, the coefficients behave
like $y_k \sim K\,Q(x)\,\Gamma(k-g)/\chi(x)^{k-g}$. At leading order this can be made exact, because the $1/n$ expansion
of $x\,{}_2F_1$ is explicit.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:4}
Let $0 < x < 1$, $\Phi$ as in \eqref{eq:Phi}, $\tau_\ell = \Phi - \iu\pi(\ell + \tfrac12)$ for $\ell \in \mathbb Z$, and
let $y = x\,{}_2F_1(\alpha,\beta;\kappa;x^2)$ with $\alpha, \beta, \kappa$ as in \eqref{eq:abk}, $y \sim \sum_k y_k\eps^k$.
If $|\Phi| < (\sqrt3/2)\pi$ (equivalently $0.066 < x < 0.998$), then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prop4}
y_k = K\,x^2(1-x^2)^{-1-s}\,\frac{\Gamma(k+s+\frac12)}{\tau_0^{\,k+s+\frac12}}\,\big(1 + O(1/k)\big), \qquad
K = e^{-\iu\pi(1+s)}\,\frac{2^{-s-1/2}}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)} .
\end{equation}
The $\ell = -1$ singularity contributes at the same rate, with a $k$-independent relative weight of $8\times10^{-5}$
($x = 0.5$) to $5\times10^{-8}$ ($x = 0.95$). If $|\Phi| > (\sqrt3/2)\pi$, the late orders are governed instead by the
$x$-independent singularities $\pm\iu\pi$.
\end{proposition}

The proof uses one elementary lemma.

\begin{lemma}[product]\label{lem:product}
Let $A = \sum_k a_k\eps^k$ and $B = \sum_j b_j\eps^j$ be Gevrey-1 series whose Borel transforms have radii of convergence
$\rho_A < \rho_B$, with $a_k$ of exact factorial growth $|a_k| \asymp k!\,\rho_A^{-k}$ (up to powers of $k$). Then
$(AB)_k = b_0a_k\,(1+O(1/k))$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$|b_ja_{k-j}/a_k| \lesssim (\rho_A/\rho_B)^j\,j!\,(k-j)!/k!$, and the sum over $j \ge 1$ of the right-hand side is
$O(1/k)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:4}]
\emph{(i) Euler integral.} For $\re\kappa > \re\beta$ (that is, $n > 1$),
${}_2F_1 = \frac{\Gamma(\kappa)}{\Gamma(\beta)\Gamma(\kappa-\beta)}\int_0^1 t^{\beta-1}(1-t)^{\kappa-\beta-1}(1-zt)^{-\alpha}\,\dd t$.
With $1 - t = e^{-2\tau}$ and $\kappa = (1 + 1/\eps)/2$,
\[
y = x\,P(\kappa)\cdot 2\int_0^\infty e^{-\tau/\eps}G(\tau)\,\dd\tau,\qquad
G = e^{(2\beta-1)\tau}(1-e^{-2\tau})^{\beta-1}(1-z+ze^{-2\tau})^{-\alpha},
\]
with $P = \Gamma(\kappa)/(\Gamma(\beta)\Gamma(\kappa-\beta))$ and $z = x^2$.

\emph{(ii) Singularities of $G$ and Watson's lemma.} Write $G = \tau^{\beta-1}H$. $H$ is analytic except where
$1 - z + ze^{-2\tau} = 0$, i.e.\ at $\tau = \tau_\ell$, each a simple zero, so that $H \propto (1-\tau/\tau_\ell)^{-\alpha}$
there; and at the branch points $\tau = \iu\pi m$, $m \ne 0$, of $(1-e^{-2\tau})^{\beta-1}$. On $\tau > 0$,
$1 - z + ze^{-2\tau} > 1 - z > 0$ and $|G| \le Ce^{\tau}$, so Watson's lemma gives
$2\int_0^\infty e^{-\tau/\eps}G \sim 2\sum_m H_m\Gamma(m+\beta)\eps^{m+\beta}$ with $H = \sum_m H_m\tau^m$.

\emph{(iii) Darboux.} The radius of convergence of $\sum_m H_m\tau^m$ is $R = \min(|\tau_0|, \pi)$. If $|\tau_0| < \pi$,
the only singularities on $|\tau| = R$ are $\tau_0$ and $\tau_{-1}$, both of type $(1-\tau/\tau_\ell)^{-\alpha}$, so
\[
H_m = \sum_{\ell=0,-1} h_\ell\,\frac{\Gamma(m+\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\,m!}\,\tau_\ell^{-m}\,\big(1+O(1/m)\big),\qquad
h_\ell = \lim_{\tau\to\tau_\ell}H(\tau)\,(1-\tau/\tau_\ell)^{\alpha},
\]
with $H$ continued along the ray from $0$.

\emph{(iv) Prefactor.} $P(\kappa)(2\eps)^{\beta}(1+\eps)^{-\beta}\Gamma(\beta) = 1 + \sum_j p_j\eps^j$, from Binet's
expansion of $\ln\Gamma(\kappa) - \ln\Gamma(\kappa-\beta)$, whose Borel transform is singular only at $\tau = \iu\pi m$;
and $(1+\eps)^{\beta}$ converges.

\emph{(v) Product.} By Lemma~\ref{lem:product}, with $b_0 = 1$ and the powers $\eps^{\pm\beta}$ cancelling,
$y_k = \frac{x\,2^{1-\beta}}{\Gamma(\beta)}\,\Gamma(k+\beta)H_k\,(1+O(1/k))$.

\emph{(vi) Combining.} $\Gamma(k+\alpha)\Gamma(k+\beta)/\Gamma(k+1) = \Gamma(k+s+\tfrac12)(1+O(1/k))$. Evaluating $h_0$ in
closed form, with $e^{2\tau_0} = z/(z-1)$, the factors reduce to $x^2(1-x^2)^{-1-s}$ times $K$, which gives
\eqref{eq:prop4}. If $|\tau_0| > \pi$, then $R = \pi$ and step (iii) applies to $\pm\iu\pi$ instead.
\end{proof}

The prediction is confirmed numerically to order $122$ at $90$ digits (\repo{solver/largeD\_prop4/}): at $x = 0.9$,
$y_k$ divided by \eqref{eq:prop4}, minus one, extrapolates to $(-0.9 - 2.3\iu)\times10^{-3}$ over $k = 100$--$122$; the
measured constant equals $K$ to $1 + 3.65/k$ at $x = 0.5$, $0.8$ and $0.9$; and at $x = 0.999$ and $0.9999$ the measured
singulant is $-3.135\iu \to -\iu\pi$, not $\Phi - \iu\pi/2$, as the statement predicts. The singularities $\pm\iu\pi$ come
from the Gamma-function prefactor and the endpoint factor $(1-e^{-2\tau})^{\beta-1}$; their exponentials $e^{\mp\iu\pi n}$
are pure phases, not exponentially small terms, so they do not enter the Stokes-line argument, which concerns the
$\tau_\ell$.

\begin{remark}[the Stokes line]\label{rem:stokesline}
Put $\chi = \tau_0 = \Phi - \iu\pi/2$. Then $e^{-n\chi} = e^{\iu n\pi/2}x^{-n}(1-x^2)^{n/2}$ is the fast mode of
Proposition~\ref{prop:3}, and $x^2(1-x^2)^{-1-s}$ is its transported amplitude at $F = 0$: the divergence of the $1/n$
series and the non-analytic piece are the same object. On the branch continued from $0 < x < 1$, $\chi$ vanishes only at
$x = \infty$ ($\Phi(\infty) = \iu\pi/2$), so the fast mode is born at the outer singular point of the equations. Its
Stokes line ($\im\chi = 0$, $\re\chi > 0$) is the real axis $x > 1$ approached from above, where
$\chi = \ln\big(x/\sqrt{x^2-1}\big)$. On $0 < x < 1$, $\im\chi = -\pi/2$, so nothing is switched on there, as
consequence (iii) of Proposition~\ref{prop:3} requires. (On the imaginary axis $\im\chi = 0$ too, but there
$\re\chi < 0$.) The Stokes line ends at the light cone, so a loop around $x = 1$ crosses it, and that is where the
branch $(1-x^2)^{n/2-1-s}$ and its coefficient come from: $C(n,s)$ is the Stokes multiplier. Directly, the $1/n$ series
truncated near its smallest term reproduces $x\,{}_2F_1$ inside the light cone far below the size of the singular branch:
at $x = 0.9$, $n = 60$ the smallest difference is $4\times10^{-40}$, against $|C|$ times the branch, $7\times10^{-21}$
(\repo{solver/largeD\_linear\_truncation.py}).
\end{remark}

\section{The \texorpdfstring{$n$}{n}-free inner problem and the selection rule}
\label{sec:inner}

\subsection{The inner equations}
Near $x = \infty$ the large-$n$ expansion is not uniform. At $x \sim \sqrt n$ one has $\eps x^2F^2 = O(1)$, so
$c - 1 = O(1)$ and gravity is no longer weak; $p \approx \eps^{1/2}xF = O(1)$; and $e^{-n\Phi} \to e^{\mp\iu n\pi/2}e^{-n/(2x^2)}$,
so fast and slow modes are no longer separated. Put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:innervars}
\xi = x/\sqrt n,\qquad y = p - m,\qquad \sigma = \sqrt n\,(p+m),\qquad b - 1 = \eta/n,\qquad
L = \xi^2\partial_\xi + \omega\xi\,\partial_\theta .
\end{equation}
At leading order the difference and the sum of \eqref{eq:E3}--\eqref{eq:E4} and the equations \eqref{eq:E12} become
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inner}
L\,y = c\,\sigma, \qquad
L\,\sigma = c\,\xi\,\partial_\xi y + c\,\big(\tfrac12 y^2 - 1\big)\,y, \qquad
\xi\,\partial_\xi c = \tfrac12\,c\,y^2 , \qquad \eta = \tfrac12 y^2 .
\end{equation}
(In the difference equation the terms of order $\sqrt n$ are $x^2\partial_x y$, $\omega x\,\partial_\theta y$ and
$nc\,(p+m)$; in the sum the terms of order $1$ are $x^2\partial_x(p+m)$, $xc\,\partial_x y$, $\omega x\,\partial_\theta(p+m)$
and $(G - c)\,y$ with $G = c\eta$; the $b$ equation becomes algebraic, $\eta = p^2 + m^2$. Lemma~\ref{lem:B50} below gives
the corrections exactly.) No large parameter is left: the inner problem is the same for every $n$. Up to a change of
variables it is the leading-order flat-space system of Rozali and Way~\cite[Sec.~2.2]{RozaliWay2018}, in the regime $r =
O(1)$, $t = O(D^{-1/2})$ where they rescale time by $\sqrt D$; the map is written out in the proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:5}(i) and verified symbolically (\repo{solver/inner\_pert/rw\_map.py}).

The formal solution regular at $\xi = 0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:innerseries}
y = \sum_{j\ge0} d_j\,\xi^{2j+1},\qquad \sigma = \sum_{j\ge0} s_j\,\xi^{2j+2},\qquad c = \sum_{j\ge0} g_j\,\xi^{2j},\qquad
d_0 = 2F,\quad g_0 = 1,
\end{equation}
is unique once $F$ is given (the coefficients follow by recursion), and it is Gevrey-1 in $\xi^2$. Its first terms are
$g_1 = F^2$, $s_0 = 2(F + \omega F')$, $d_1 = \omega^2F'' + 3\omega F' + 2F - 2F^3$: the top-degree coefficients of $G_1$,
$S_1$ and $D_1$ in \eqref{eq:firstorders}. In general $d_k$, $-g_k$ and $s_{k-1}$ are the top-degree coefficients of the
outer $D_k$, $G_k$ and $S_k$ (numerically to $2\times10^{-13}$ at orders $1$--$10$, for $F = 2.72\sin\theta$ and
$\omega = \winf$; \repo{solver/largeD\_inner.py}): the inner problem resums the outer series' top-degree terms. The free
functions $K_k$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:1} multiply $x$, the lowest degree, so they never reach the inner problem, which
contains $F$ and $\omega$ only.

\subsection{The linear inner problem}
With $t = 1/\xi$ and the characteristic coordinate $\phi = \theta + \omega\ln t$, one has $L = -\partial_t$ at fixed
$\phi$, and eliminating $\sigma$ from \eqref{eq:inner} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:oscillator}
y'' + \Big(c^2t - \frac{c'}{c}\Big)\,y' + c^2\Big[\omega\,\partial_\phi y + \big(1 - \tfrac12 y^2\big)\,y\Big] = 0,
\qquad t\,c' + \omega\,\partial_\phi c = -\tfrac12\,c\,y^2,
\end{equation}
with $' = \partial_t$ at fixed $\phi$: along each characteristic, a softening anharmonic oscillator with friction $c^2t$.
Without gravity ($c = 1$) and for one harmonic, $w = t^{-s}y_\mu$ obeys Hermite's equation $w'' + tw' + (1+s)\,w = 0$, and
$w = e^{-t^2/4}v$ gives Weber's equation $v'' + (\tfrac12 + s - \tfrac14t^2)\,v = 0$. The solution regular at $\xi = 0$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:2F0}
y_\mu = 2F_\mu\,\xi\,{}_2F_0(\alpha,\beta;;2\xi^2),\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad d_j = 2^j\,\frac{(\alpha)_j(\beta)_j}{j!}\,d_0 ,
\end{equation}
and the second solution carries $e^{-1/(2\xi^2)}$, which is the outer fast mode $e^{-n/(2x^2)}$. The Stokes line of
${}_2F_0(\cdot\,;2\xi^2)$ is the real $\xi$ axis, i.e.\ the real axis $x > 1$. The lateral solutions are
$y^\pm_\mu = 2F_\mu\,\xi\,X^{\alpha}U(\alpha,\tfrac12,X)$, $X = -1/(2\xi^2)$, with $y^+$ on the upper side of the cut of
Kummer's $U$ ($\arg X \to \pi^-$) and $y^-$ on the lower side~\cite[\S\S2.11, 13.7]{DLMF}. Their difference on real
$\xi$ is $2\pi\iu\,K'(s)\,2F_\mu$ times the recessive (fast) solution, with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Kprime}
K'(s) = \frac{2^{-s-1/2}}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\Gamma(1+s)}
\end{equation}
by the duplication formula; this normalization is checked in ball arithmetic to $1 \pm 3\times10^{-30}$ at two radii and
two frequencies (\repo{solver/proof/lin\_check.py}). Matching the inner fast mode to the outer one gives the large-$n$
form of the light-cone coefficient of Proposition~\ref{prop:2}, with $\Lambda = n/2 - 1 - s$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Clarge}
C(n,s) = -2\pi\iu\,K'(s)\,(n+1)^{s+1/2}\,e^{-\iu\pi\Lambda}\,\big(1 + O(s^2/n)\big)
= -\iu\,\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\Gamma(1+s)}\,(n+1)^{s+1/2}\,e^{-\iu\pi\Lambda}\,\big(1 + O(s^2/n)\big),
\end{equation}
Weber's classical Stokes constant $\sqrt{2\pi}/\Gamma(-\nu_W)$ with $\nu_W = -1-s$~\cite[\S12.9]{DLMF}. The ratio of the two
sides is $1.00014$ at $n = 10^5$ for $\mu = 1$ ($1.0013$ for $\mu = 3$), with $n\,(\text{ratio} - 1) \to 13.9 - 7.6\iu$, of
the size of $|s^2| = 14.6$ (\repo{solver/largeD\_stokes\_C.py}); at $D = 20$--$60$ the ratio is still $2.1$--$1.27$, since
the asymptotics need $\mu^2\omega^2 \ll n$. So, for large $n$, the light-cone coefficient is the $n$-independent Stokes
constant $K'(s)$ of a ${}_2F_0$, times $(n+1)^{s+1/2}$ and a phase. In the linear case the outer late-order constant $K$ of
Proposition~\ref{prop:4} and this inner Stokes constant are the same object; this is the exact linear version of the
inner--outer matching.

\subsection{The fast mode with gravity, and the multipliers}
With $\delta y = e^{-1/(2\xi^2)}a$ in the linearization of \eqref{eq:inner}, the terms of order $\xi^{-2}$ cancel and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fastinner}
\xi\,\partial_\xi a + 2\omega\,\partial_\theta a = 2F^2a ,
\end{equation}
which is the large-$x$ limit of \eqref{eq:transport}. Its solutions are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:label}
a(\xi,\theta) = \xi^{2\langle F^2\rangle}\,e^{\Pi(\theta)}\,g(\theta_0),\qquad \theta_0 = \theta - 2\omega\ln\xi,\qquad
\Pi(\theta) = \frac1\omega\int^\theta\big(F^2 - \langle F^2\rangle\big),
\end{equation}
with $g$ an arbitrary $2\pi$-periodic function of the characteristic label $\theta_0$. Accordingly the jump of the inner
solution across the Stokes line (real $\xi$), the difference of the lateral sums along $\arg\xi^2 = 0^\pm$, has the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jump}
[y](\xi,\theta) \sim S(\theta_0)\,e^{-1/(2\xi^2)}\,\xi^{2\langle F^2\rangle}\,e^{\Pi(\theta)}\,\big(1 + O(\xi^2)\big),
\qquad \xi\to0^+,\qquad S(\theta_0) = \sum_{\nu\ \rm odd} S_\nu\,e^{\iu\nu\theta_0} .
\end{equation}
In the Borel plane conjugate to $1/\xi^2$ the singularity stays at $\zeta = \tfrac12$ (without gravity the inner series
grows at ratio $d_{j+1}/d_j \sim 2j$), and the Dingle exponents become $g_\nu = -(\tfrac12 + \iu\nu\omega) + \langle F^2\rangle$:
the late orders of label harmonic $\nu$ grow like $\Gamma(j - g_\nu)$, each spread over the harmonics $\nu + 2k$ by
$e^{\Pi}$. Darboux fits with these exponents leave residuals of $5\times10^{-8}$ in the one-harmonic model.

\begin{definition}[fast-type Stokes multipliers]\label{def:mult}
For a model with finitely many harmonics, the formal series \eqref{eq:innerseries} is $1$-summable in every direction
except $\arg\xi^2 = 0$ (Braaksma's theorem~\cite{Braaksma1992}; Lemma~\ref{lem:B4} below). The two lateral sums differ
on $\xi > 0$ by an exponentially small solution of the variational equation, and the coefficients of its first term in
the basis $e^{-1/(2\xi^2)}\xi^{2\langle F^2\rangle}e^{\Pi(\theta)}e^{\iu\nu\theta_0}$ are the multipliers $S_\nu(F,\omega)$.
For the untruncated problem the multipliers are defined order by order in an amplitude expansion
(Section~\ref{sec:amp}); a definition directly in a space of $\theta$-analytic functions is part of gaps (a) and (b)
below.
\end{definition}

The selection conditions are $S_\nu = 0$ for every odd $\nu$: one complex condition per odd harmonic, for the unknowns
$F$'s odd harmonics (less one phase) and $\omega$, so the count balances. Two features make the problem harder than its
scalar precedents. First, gravity couples the fast modes of different harmonics, so the multiplier is a vector indexed
by harmonics with a matrix structure. Second, the linear multipliers grow with the harmonic: $S^{\rm lin}_\nu$ is
proportional to $F_\nu/(\Gamma(\alpha_\nu)\Gamma(\beta_\nu))$, and $1/|\Gamma(\alpha_\nu)\Gamma(\beta_\nu)| \approx 47$ for
$\nu = 1$ and $4.4\times10^6$ for $\nu = 3$, growing like $e^{\pi|\nu|\omega/2}$ with $\pi\winf/2 = 6.004$. So $S(\theta_0)$
formally involves $F$ continued to $\im\theta = \mp\pi\omega/2 \approx \mp6$, while $F$'s harmonics decay only like
$e^{-1.4|\nu|}$, and a harmonic generated only by the nonlinearity already carries a jump $10^2$--$10^4$ times that of
harmonic $1$. Truncating harmonics is therefore not a controlled approximation; Section~\ref{sec:tail} explains why the
weights nevertheless cancel in the tuned problem.

\subsection{The selection rule}

\begin{conjecture}[selection at $D = \infty$]\label{conj}
For $n = D - 2$ let $F_n(\theta) = \sqrt n\,\partial_xp(0,\theta)$ and $\omega_n = 2\pi/\Delta(D)$ be the profile and the
frequency of the critical solution. Assume
\begin{enumerate}[label=(H\arabic*),leftmargin=3em]
\item $F_n \to F_\infty$ uniformly on a strip $|\im\theta| < \sigma_F$ for some $\sigma_F > 0$, and $\omega_n \to \winf > 0$
(the data give $\sigma_F \approx 1.4$, the harmonic decay rate); and
\item the formal family of Proposition~\ref{prop:1} with data $(F_n, \omega_n, K_k)$ is the asymptotic expansion of the
solution uniformly on $\delta \le x \le 1 - \delta$ (checked: residuals $\propto n^{-3}$ through third order).
\end{enumerate}
Then $S_\nu(F_\infty, \winf) = 0$ for every odd $\nu$, where $S_\nu$ are the fast-type Stokes multipliers of the inner
problem \eqref{eq:inner}. Conversely, a zero of $S$ at which the Jacobian with respect to $(F, \omega)$, modulo the
phase, is invertible should continue to critical solutions for all large $n$.
\end{conjecture}

\subsubsection*{The argument}
The inner problem resums the outer series' top-degree terms; the outer series' light-cone exponential is fixed by the
inner singularity at $x = \infty$ (exactly so in the linear case, where the outer late-order constant of
Proposition~\ref{prop:4} is the inner Weber constant); the switched exponential reaches the light cone through an
invertible map; and analyticity at the light cone forces $C = 0$, hence $S = 0$. The map $S \mapsto C$ is, at leading
order in $1/n$, the composition of three maps:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),leftmargin=2em]
\item \emph{Inner--outer matching} on $1 \ll x \ll \sqrt n$, where the inner fast mode
$e^{-1/(2\xi^2)}\xi^{2\langle F^2\rangle}e^{\Pi(\theta)}S(\theta_0)$ matches the outer WKB mode $e^{-n\Phi}A$ on the branch above
the real axis $x > 1$ (there $\Phi = \ln(x/\sqrt{x^2-1}) + \iu\pi/2$).
\item \emph{The outer transport} of Proposition~\ref{prop:3} along $x > 1$ with real $\theta$. It has real coefficients
while $\eps x^2F^2 \ll 1$ and acts along real characteristics as multiplication by a non-vanishing function composed with a
shift, so it is invertible on Fourier sequences of at most exponential growth.
\item \emph{The half-monodromy} at the regular singular point $x = 1$. It continues the singular Floquet branch
$(1-x)^{\Lambda}\varphi(\theta - \omega'\ln(1-x))$, $\omega' = \omega/(1 - \langle G(1)\rangle)$, from $x > 1$ to $x < 1$ and
multiplies harmonic $\mu$ by $e^{-\iu\pi\Lambda}e^{\pm\pi\mu\omega'}$: a diagonal map with non-zero entries.
\end{enumerate}
So $C \equiv 0 \Leftrightarrow S \equiv 0$ at leading order. In the linear case this is exact
(Propositions~\ref{prop:2} and~\ref{prop:4} with \eqref{eq:Clarge}).

\subsubsection*{Where rigour fails}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=2em]
\item \emph{Borel summability.} Borel summability of the nonlinear series is not proved. The outer nonlinear late
orders also carry $x$-independent singularities of modulus about $0.13$, driven by high harmonics, and one must show
that they give only ambiguities in the free data ($F$'s harmonics, $K_k$), not light-cone branches. (In the linear case
the analogous singularities $\pm\iu\pi$ are harmless pure phases; that is suggestive, not a proof.)
\item \emph{Uniformity in the harmonic index.} The weights $e^{\pi|\mu|\omega/2}$ grow like $e^{6.0|\mu|}$ while $F$'s
harmonics decay only like $e^{-1.4|\mu|}$, so an $O(1/n)$ relative error in a high harmonic could be amplified when it
mixes into low harmonics through $e^{\Pi}$. The leading-order equivalence holds harmonic by harmonic, but the $n$ at which
it becomes accurate might grow with the number of harmonics.
\item \emph{Uniformity at $x = 1$.} The outer WKB amplitude is singular there, $A \propto (1-x)^{-1}$, and must be matched
to the Frobenius solution on $1/n \ll |1-x| \ll 1$. The exponents agree, $\Lambda = n/2 - 1 - s + \langle F^2\rangle + O(1/n)$
on both sides, but the matching must be written out.
\end{enumerate}
Section~\ref{sec:layerB} shows how far each gap is closed for a model with finitely many harmonics: (c) holds for each fixed
number of harmonics up to a routine block-diagonalization, by a Volterra bound that is proved (Lemma~\ref{lem:B6p}); the
inner half of (a) is Braaksma's theorem, and the outer half rests on two hypotheses checked numerically; and (b) is
narrower than stated here (Section~\ref{sec:tail}).

\section{An exact unstable mode at infinite dimension}
\label{sec:prop5}

By Remark~\ref{rem:beyond} the growth rate $\lambda = 1/\gamma$ of a perturbation $\propto e^{\lambda\tau}$ is, like $\omega$,
selected beyond all orders in $1/n$, by the same kind of fast-mode multiplier. In the inner problem, however, some growth
rates are exact, because they come from symmetries: $\lambda = 0$ (a shift of $\theta$), $\lambda = 1$ (a shift of the time
of the singularity, $\delta Z = (\xi\partial_\xi + \omega\partial_\theta)Z$ for $Z = (y,\sigma,c)$, which is the $D = \infty$
limit of the gauge mode of the solver), and $\lambda = 2$, the value the numerical growth rates approach. This section
derives the last.

Write the inner problem in physical inner variables: $\rho = r/\sqrt n$, with $r$ the areal radius, and the time $t$.
Then $\xi = \rho/(-t)$, $L = \rho\,\partial_t$ at fixed $\rho$, and $\xi\partial_\xi = \rho\partial_\rho$ at fixed $t$. Put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:heatvars}
s = \frac{\rho^2}{2} = \frac{r^2}{2n},\qquad y = \sqrt s\,Y,\qquad \sigma = \sqrt2\,s\,W .
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:5}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2em]
\item In the variables \eqref{eq:heatvars} the inner equations \eqref{eq:inner} are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:heat}
Y_t = c\,W, \qquad W_t = c\,\Big(Y_s + \frac{Y^3}{4}\Big), \qquad c_s = \frac{c\,Y^2}{4},
\end{equation}
that is, the nonlinear heat equation $Y_s = c^{-1}\partial_t(c^{-1}\partial_tY) - Y^3/4$ in which $s$ plays the role of time
and $t$ that of space. The system contains no explicit $s$ or $t$, so it is invariant under $s$-translation,
$t$-translation, the scaling $(t, s, Y, W) \mapsto (at, a^2s, Y/a, W/a^2)$ and the reparametrizations $t \mapsto T(t)$,
$c \mapsto T'c$.
\item Let $(y, \sigma, c)(\xi, \theta)$ be a discretely self-similar solution of \eqref{eq:inner} with frequency $\omega$,
regular at $\xi = 0$, with $y = 2F\xi + O(\xi^3)$ and $c = 1 + F^2\xi^2 + O(\xi^4)$. Let $P(\theta)$ be the periodic
solution of $P + \omega P' = -2F^2$, and $X = \xi\partial_\xi + \omega\partial_\theta$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mode2}
\delta y = e^{2\tau}\Big(\frac{\xi y_\xi - y}{\xi^2} + P\,Xy\Big), \quad
\delta\sigma = e^{2\tau}\Big(\frac{\xi \sigma_\xi - 2\sigma}{\xi^2} + P\,X\sigma\Big), \quad
\delta c = e^{2\tau}\Big(\frac{c_\xi}{\xi} + (P + \omega P')\,c + P\,Xc\Big)
\end{equation}
solves the inner equations linearized about $(y, \sigma, c)$: it is an exact perturbation with growth rate $\lambda = 2$.
It is regular at $\xi = 0$ ($\delta y$ odd and $\delta c$ even in $\xi$), it keeps $c = 1$ at the centre ($\delta c = 0$ at
$\xi = 0$), and its leading profile is $\delta y = 2f\xi e^{2\tau} + O(\xi^3)$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:f}
f = (2 + \omega\partial_\theta)(F + \omega F') - 2F^3 + P\,(F + \omega F') .
\end{equation}
\item Wherever the fast-type Stokes multipliers of the background are defined (by Borel summation; gap (a)), the
multipliers of this perturbation are the image of the background's under a linear map, and they vanish when those
vanish. So if the $D = \infty$ critical solution is selected by Conjecture~\ref{conj}, $\lambda = 2$ is one of its exact
growth rates.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) With $\rho\partial_\rho = 2s\partial_s$ the explicit factors of $\rho$ cancel; this is a direct substitution, done
symbolically with zero residual (\repo{solver/inner\_pert/heat\_form.py}). No coefficient depends on $s$ or $t$, which
gives the translations. Under the scaling every term of each equation picks up the same power of $a$. Under
$t \mapsto T(t)$, $Y$ and $W$ are scalars and $\partial_t$ picks up $T'$, which $c \mapsto T'c$ absorbs.

\emph{The same system as Rozali and Way's.} Their metric is $-(1-\delta)A\,\dd t^2 + \dd r^2/A + r^2\dd\Omega^2$, with
$\tau_{\rm RW} = \sqrt D\,t$, $A = 1 - A_0/D$ and $\delta = \delta_0$ at leading order. Their leading-order equations
$\partial_r\delta_0 = -2r(\partial_{\tau_{\rm RW}}\phi_0)^2$ and
$\partial_r\phi_0 = r(1-\delta_0)^{-1/2}\partial_{\tau_{\rm RW}}[(1-\delta_0)^{-1/2}\partial_{\tau_{\rm RW}}\phi_0]$ are posed at $r = O(1)$ and
times of order $D^{-1/2}$, which is the inner region. With their $(r, \tau_{\rm RW})$ identified with our $(\rho, t)$ and
their $1 - \delta_0$ with our $c^2$, they read $\phi_s = c^{-1}\partial_t(c^{-1}\phi_t)$ and $c_s = \phi_t^2/c$. Then
$Y = 2\phi_t/c$ and $W = Y_t/c$ satisfy \eqref{eq:heat} identically (\repo{solver/inner\_pert/rw\_map.py}); the coefficient
$1/4$ of the cubic term is not adjusted but comes out of the map.

(ii) A generator of weight $k$ under the scaling, applied to a self-similar solution, gives a perturbation
$\propto e^{k\tau}$. With $\tau = -\ln(-t)$, $\partial_t$ at fixed $\rho$ is $e^{\tau}(\partial_\tau + \xi\partial_\xi)$,
which is $e^\tau X$ on a DSS background, and $\partial_s = \rho^{-1}\partial_\rho = e^{2\tau}\xi^{-2}\,\xi\partial_\xi$ at fixed
$t$. So $s$-translation, $(\delta Y, \delta W, \delta c) = (Y_s, W_s, c_s)$, has weight $2$ and gives the first terms of
\eqref{eq:mode2}; for instance $\sqrt{s}\,\partial_s(y/\sqrt{s}) = \rho^{-2}(\rho y_\rho - y)$. At $\xi = 0$ it changes $c$
by $\lim c_\xi/\xi = 2F^2$ per unit of $e^{2\tau}$, which the normalization $c(t, 0) = 1$ forbids. The infinitesimal
reparametrization $t \mapsto t - \eta(t)$ with $\eta = e^{\tau}P(\theta)$ moves the scalars by
$\eta\,\partial_t = e^{2\tau}P X$ and moves $c$ by that plus $\eta' c = e^{2\tau}(P + \omega P')\,c$. At $\xi = 0$, where
$Xc = 0$, the two changes of $c$ cancel exactly when $P + \omega P' = -2F^2$. Harmonic by harmonic this reads
$(1 + \iu\nu\omega)P_\nu = -2(F^2)_\nu$, and $1 + \iu\nu\omega \ne 0$, so $P$ exists, is unique and is as smooth as $F^2$. A
symmetry maps solutions to solutions, so its generator solves the linearized equations. For regularity, write
$y = \sum_j d_j\xi^{2j+1}$ and $c = \sum_m g_m\xi^{2m}$: the $s$-translation part is
$\sum_{k\ge0} 2(k+1)\,d_{k+1}\,\xi^{2k+1}$ for $y$ and $\sum_{m\ge0} 2(m+1)\,g_{m+1}\,\xi^{2m}$ for $c$, and $X$ preserves parity in
$\xi$. The leading coefficient is $2d_1 + 2P(F + \omega F')$, with $d_1 = \omega^2F'' + 3\omega F' + 2F - 2F^3$, which is
\eqref{eq:f}.

(iii) Write the perturbation as $\delta Z = \mathcal D Z$, where $\mathcal D$ is a first-order differential operator in
$(\xi, \theta)$ whose coefficients ($\xi^{-2}$, $\xi^{-1}$, $P(\theta)$, $P'(\theta)$ and constants, times $e^{2\tau}$) are
analytic away from $\xi = 0$. The formal solution regular at $\xi = 0$ with a given leading profile and $\delta c(0) = 0$
is unique (the same recursion as for the background), so the formal series of $\delta Z$ is $\mathcal D$ applied to the
background's. Lateral Borel--Laplace sums commute with differentiation and with multiplication by convergent series, so
the jump of $\delta Z$ across the Stokes line is $\mathcal D$ applied to the background's jump, which is zero when the
background's multipliers vanish.
\end{proof}

The perturbation recursion of the repository (gravity included, $\delta c(0) = 0$ imposed, nothing about symmetries built
in), run at $\lambda = 2$ from the profile \eqref{eq:f}, reproduces the predicted coefficients to $10^{-180}$ relative in
every harmonic the Galerkin truncation resolves, for $F = 2.72\sin\theta + 0.15\sin3\theta$, through $j = 11$ at $45$
harmonics (\repo{solver/inner\_pert/pert\_check3.py 45 11}); with $\lambda = 1.9$ or $2.1$ the same $f$ misses by $7.5\%$ at
the third order. The modes $\lambda = 0$ and $\lambda = 1$ pass the same kind of check, to $6\times10^{-30}$ and (with
gravity) $10^{-177}$. Two remarks on the structure: the self-similar variable of a heat equation is $t/\sqrt s$, here a
multiple of $1/\xi$, and the linear inner problem is indeed Hermite's equation; and the inner fast mode
$e^{-1/(2\xi^2)} = e^{-t^2/(4s)}$ is the heat kernel.

The mode is physical, not gauge. The reparametrization is gauge, and $t$-translation ($\lambda = 1$) shifts the
accumulation time, but $s$-translation shifts $r^2$, and $r$ is the areal radius. A supercritical evolution therefore
forms its horizon at $r_{\rm BH}^2 \propto p - p^*$, which is the discretely self-similar counterpart of Rozali and Way's
estimate $\gamma = 1/2$ from the divergence of their oscillating solutions~\cite{RozaliWay2018}.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:gamma}
Suppose that Conjecture~\ref{conj} holds, that the $D = \infty$ critical solution has no unstable mode other than
$\lambda = 2$ and the gauge mode $\lambda = 1$, and that $\lambda(D)$ is continuous at $1/n = 0$. Then
$\gamma_\infty = 1/2$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:5}(iii) and Conjecture~\ref{conj}, $\lambda = 2$ is a growth rate of the $D = \infty$ critical
solution, and by the second hypothesis it is its only physical unstable mode. By continuity the growth rate $\lambda(D)$
of the unstable mode tends to $2$, and $\gamma = 1/\lambda \to 1/2$.
\end{proof}

At finite $D$ the outer region and the $1/n$ corrections to the inner equations depend on $r$ explicitly
(Lemma~\ref{lem:B50}), so the symmetry is broken and the mode survives as an ordinary unstable mode with
$\lambda = 2 + O(1/n)$; the data give $\lambda = 2 + 3.0324/n + \dots$, with the $1/n$ coefficient stable to about
$10^{-3}$ over fit choices (3.0313--3.0324)~\cite{companion}. Two questions are open: whether $\lambda = 2$ is the only
physical unstable mode at $D = \infty$, and the coefficient $3.0324$, which by first-order perturbation theory is
$-S_{1/n}/\partial_\lambda S$ at $\lambda = 2$, with $S$ the perturbation's fast-mode multiplier and $S_{1/n}$ the part induced
by the $O(1/n)$ inner equations. The one-harmonic model of Section~\ref{sec:thmA} has a root $\lambda = 1.765$; this is not a
counterexample, because its background is not the $D = \infty$ solution and because Galerkin truncation does not commute
with the gauge step, which multiplies by $P(\theta)$.
